\title{Body Pose Does Not Improve Pedestrian Trajectory Forecasting,\\
and the Tracker Already Explains Why}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Erik Sch\"utz}
\IEEEauthorblockA{\textit{(affiliation)}\\
\textit{(institution)}\\
(city, country) \\
erik.schuetz@gmx.de}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Body pose is an intuitively attractive input for pedestrian trajectory
forecasting: gait, stance and facing appear to encode intent that a position
history does not. We test this on the Waymo Open Motion Dataset with SMPL poses
recovered from LiDAR, and find no measurable benefit. The decisive comparison is
not pose against no pose---which confounds body information with the capacity of
an extra input branch---but correct pose against the same architecture fed
\emph{another pedestrian's} pose. On that control the effect is
$+0.66\%$ ($p{=}0.46$) from scratch, $+0.88\%$ ($p{=}0.11$) after full-dataset
pretraining, and $+0.04\%$ ($p{=}0.97$) at $40\times$ more training data, while
the baseline improves $19\%$ from data alone. A directed search over the
integration code accepted none of 20 candidates, and a pre-registered eight-seed
confirmation on a held-out split returned $-0.04\%$ ($p{=}0.92$).
We then explain the null rather than report it. Body facing does predict which way
a standing pedestrian steps off, at roughly twice chance---but the tracker's box
heading estimates the same quantity more accurately ($25.2^\circ$ versus
$44.2^\circ$ median error), is already an input to the model, and is not beaten by
pose in any range, occlusion or heading-jitter stratum; the two degrade together
because both derive from the same returns. Head orientation, the one potentially
complementary cue, is not recovered from LiDAR at all. Finally, we show that
aggregate MPJPE is the wrong acceptance metric for pose in a perception stack:
scored per joint against a mean-pose baseline, the head carries a tenth of the
skill of any limb joint while its MPJPE looks \emph{better} than either wrist.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Predicting where a pedestrian will go is a safety-critical function of an
automated vehicle, and body pose is an obvious candidate input. A person's stance,
gait phase and facing direction plausibly reveal intent before their position
changes, and modern perception stacks already produce human mesh or keypoint
estimates. Several recent systems condition trajectory forecasts on pose.

This paper reports that, on the Waymo Open Motion Dataset (WOMD), pose
conditioning does not measurably improve pedestrian trajectory accuracy---and,
more usefully for practitioners, identifies the specific reason, which is a
property of the perception stack rather than of the model.

We make three contributions.

\textbf{A controlled null.} We measure pose conditioning against a shuffled-pose
control: the same architecture receiving a different pedestrian's body motion.
This isolates information content from the capacity cost of an extra branch, and
it is the comparison a practitioner should demand before adopting any auxiliary
modality. The control is null in every regime, at three training scales, across
six pose representations and two integration styles, under a directed
architecture search, and in a pre-registered eight-seed confirmation on a held-out
split.

\textbf{A mechanism.} Body facing is genuinely predictive for the one case where
a position history is uninformative---a stationary pedestrian about to step
off---but the object tracker already estimates facing, more accurately than mesh
fitting recovers it, and already supplies it to the model as a heading channel.
Pose is therefore not adding information; it is duplicating a channel, less well.
We show this holds at every range, occlusion level and tracker-confidence
stratum, because both estimates derive from the same LiDAR returns.

\textbf{A measurement caveat.} Aggregate MPJPE cannot distinguish a pose
estimator that tracks a joint imprecisely from one that ignores the input and
emits its prior. We score each joint against an always-predict-the-mean-pose
baseline and find that MPJPE rank-orders joints roughly backwards with respect to
information content. Anyone accepting a pose estimator into an AV stack on a
single error number is at risk of importing a prior.

We also report, as a data-integrity cautionary note, that our own first attempt at
this study was invalidated by an export bug that silently substituted other
pedestrians' bodies; the mechanism is one that any pose pipeline keyed by object
identity can reproduce.

\section{Related Work}
Trajectory forecasting on WOMD is dominated by anchor- and query-based
transformers; we build on MTR~\cite{shi2022mtr,shi2023mtrpp}, which is
representative and strong. Pose-conditioned prediction has been explored mainly on
pedestrian-centric datasets with clean
poses~\cite{saadatnejad2024socialtransmotion,gao2025socialpose,salzmann2023robots},
where pose is an input rather than an estimate. Human mesh recovery from LiDAR is
maturing~\cite{fan2023lidarhmr}, with motion priors used to complete sparse
observations~\cite{rempe2021humor,he2022nemf,mahmood2019amass}. Intent-focused
work on pedestrian datasets~\cite{rasouli2019pie,fang2018pedestrian} targets
crossing decisions rather than metric trajectories. Our contribution is not a new
architecture but a controlled measurement of whether the modality pays at all in
an AV setting, together with the reason it does not.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Data and a data-integrity failure}
We use WOMD~\cite{ettinger2021waymo} with SMPL~\cite{loper2015smpl} poses
recovered from LiDAR by a LiDAR-HMR~\cite{fan2023lidarhmr} pipeline with motion-prior
completion. Poses cover the $11$ observed frames; the prediction horizon is
$8$\,s at $10$\,Hz.

Our first pass at this study was invalid. The export keyed pose clips by a
scene-\emph{local} object identifier across ${\sim}326$k scenes, so clips
collided across scenes: nearly every training sequence carried another
pedestrian's body motion, and $4.7\times$ of the pedestrians were dropped.
A per-scene re-export ($299{,}220$ scenes, $1.81$M sequences) cut the
median error between root heading and travel direction from $41.5^\circ$ to
$11.4^\circ$ and raised the gait--speed correlation from $r{=}0.26$ to $0.58$.
We report this because the failure is silent---training converges, losses look
normal---and because identity-keyed joins are common in pose pipelines. Every
number in this paper is from the corrected export.

\subsection{Arms and the content test}
Three arms share one architecture. \emph{No-pose} receives trajectories and map.
\emph{Pose} adds the pedestrian's own SMPL sequence. \emph{Shuffled} receives
another pedestrian's sequence, with identical parameter count, optimiser and
input dimensionality.

Pose versus no-pose confounds information with branch capacity; pose versus
shuffled isolates content. We call the latter the \emph{content test} and treat it
as decisive. Relative differences are $(A-B)/B$; negative favours $A$. The primary
test is a hierarchical bootstrap resampling seeds then pedestrians; a
pedestrian-only bootstrap ignores between-seed variance and is anti-conservative.
Validation is fixed at $5{,}171$ scenes and $16{,}486$ pedestrians.

\subsection{The null, and its bound}
Table~\ref{tab:ladder} trains all three arms at three scales. Data alone improves
the baseline by $19.2\%$; the content test is null at every rung. At $25$k the
bootstrap interval on the absolute content difference is
$[-0.0040,+0.0047]$\,m---about $\pm1.2\%$---so at the largest scale we train, the
data exclude a content effect beyond roughly $1.2\%$ in either direction.

After pretraining a trajectory-only backbone on full WOMD and fine-tuning
($n{=}5$), the content test is $+0.88\%$ ($p{=}0.11$), again with its sign against
pose (Table~\ref{tab:core}).

Seed count decides what Table~\ref{tab:core} appears to say. Using only the first
three seeds, the same runs read as \emph{pose harms} from scratch ($+1.85\%$,
$p{=}0.037$) and \emph{shuffled is best} after fine-tuning ($-1.27\%$,
$p{=}0.016$); both dissolve at $n{=}5$. The worst single run in the table---seed
$404$'s no-pose run at $0.495$---lies outside the three-seed set, and its absence
flattered the baseline. Three seeds do not resolve a $1\%$ effect at this scale,
which is worth stating because three is a common budget.

\subsection{Two effects that are real, and are not pose}
\textbf{A branch-capacity tax.} At $25$k both pose arms are significantly worse
than no-pose ($+1.66\%$ and $+1.62\%$) and indistinguishable from each other. An
extra input branch costs ${\sim}1.6\%$ whether it carries the right body or the
wrong one. Replacing the $144$-d branch with eight frame-invariant descriptors
fused into the existing agent channels removes the cost entirely ($-0.01\%$
versus no-pose, $p{=}0.999$; $-1.66\%$ versus the branch, $p{=}0.006$) without
creating a benefit: pose can be carried for free, and still does not pay.

\textbf{Tuning dominates the treatment.} At $5$k, the learning rate inherited
from the smaller rung made pose look catastrophic ($+37\%$); at an intermediate
rate, $+7.4\%$; with per-arm tuning and full training, $-0.90\%$, n.s. Across the
$25$k grid the learning rate moves \minADE by up to $16.7\%$ against a content
signal of ${\sim}0.05\%$. A pose-versus-baseline comparison that does not tune
each arm separately is a claim about the learning rate.

\subsection{Search and confirmation}
Hand-designed integrations sample a small space, so we ran a directed search in
which a coding agent rewrote the pose-integration code against a frozen training
repository, scoring on a development split and never seeing a held-out split.
Two rounds and $20$ scored candidates accepted none.

The best candidate reached $-1.21\%$ on the held-out split under a truncated
$12$-epoch proxy. We pre-registered a confirmation---three arms, eight seeds,
$30$ epochs, primary endpoint the held-out split at the fixed last epoch---and it
returned $-0.04\%$, $t(7){=}-0.11$, $p{=}0.92$. The proxy signal was a
convergence-rate artifact: the arms differ in how fast they train, not where they
end.

\subsection{Where a benefit could still hide}
On the confirmation runs, the content test is null at every horizon and least
favourable at the shortest ($+0.48\%$ at $2$\,s, $-0.04\%$ at $8$\,s); null in
all $20$ tests across quartiles and top deciles of constant-velocity residual,
heading variation, speed range and peak acceleration; and null for
standing-then-moving pedestrians on the full validation set ($n{=}1{,}809$,
$+0.38\%$, $p{=}0.80$)---the subpopulation constructed to favour it.

\section{Why Pose Does Not Pay}
\label{sec:why}

\subsection{The informative cue is already an input}
If body configuration predicts anything a trajectory does not, it is which way a
stationary pedestrian will step off---the one case where velocity carries no
directional information. We isolated this as a task: every pedestrian stationary
at prediction time ($<0.3$\,m/s), $224{,}946$ in training and $19{,}939$ in
validation, of whom $8.5\%$ move more than $1$\,m within $3$\,s.

SMPL body facing predicts onset direction at roughly twice chance: median error
$44.2^\circ$, $50.6\%$ within $45^\circ$ (chance: $90^\circ$, $25\%$).

The tracker does it better. Waymo's per-object box heading gives $25.2^\circ$ and
$63.9\%$. Paired on the same events, SMPL is $7.3^\circ$ worse in median (Wilcoxon
$p{=}2.6{\times}10^{-43}$) and better on only $35\%$ of events---even though we
calibrated the SMPL forward axis \emph{against} the box heading first, which
favours pose. That heading is already an input channel to the predictor.

Crucially, pose does not take over where the tracker degrades
(Table~\ref{tab:strata}). The gap is positive in all twelve strata we tested
(every $p<10^{-5}$) and \emph{widens} with range and occlusion. Both estimates
derive from the same LiDAR returns, so they fail together rather than
complementarily. Nor does pose add anything on top: a model predicting onset
direction from box heading alone reaches $24.8^\circ$; adding a full pose
descriptor gives $26.5^\circ$, and adding a \emph{shuffled} descriptor gives
$25.8^\circ$---the degradation is the cost of extra dimensions, not a property of
pose.

\subsection{The complementary cue is not measured}
Gaze precedes locomotion, so head yaw relative to the torso is a plausible
independent cue. It is not recoverable here. In the fitted meshes head-versus-torso
yaw has a standard deviation of $3.8^\circ$; against ground-truth 3D keypoints on
Waymo Perception ($n{=}8{,}234$) the real quantity varies $19.2^\circ$. The
estimator is emitting its prior. Physically, a pedestrian at $20$--$40$\,m returns
a few dozen points, and the head is a near-spherical handful of them with little
geometry to constrain yaw.

\subsection{MPJPE is the wrong acceptance metric}
A single mean error cannot distinguish an estimator that tracks a joint
imprecisely from one that emits its prior. We therefore score each joint against
the strongest trivial competitor, always predicting the mean root-aligned pose:
$\mathrm{SKILL}_j = 1 - \mathrm{MPJPE}_j / \mathrm{MP}_j$, where values at or
below zero mean the estimator contributes nothing at that joint.

Evaluated on Waymo Perception against ground-truth keypoints ($1{,}789$ frames,
$534$ pedestrians), every limb joint scores $+0.457$ to $+0.639$ (Table~\ref{tab:skill}). The head
scores $+0.045$---yet its MPJPE ($111.6$\,mm) is \emph{better} than either wrist
($118.3$/$125.4$\,mm). Its error is flattering not because it is well estimated
but because it barely improves on its own mean-pose baseline ($116.9$\,mm),
whereas the hips---whose ground-truth spread is \emph{smaller} at $97.9$\,mm---are
predicted at $39.8$\,mm. MPJPE rank-orders these joints roughly backwards
with respect to information content. We recommend reporting per-joint skill
alongside it whenever a pose estimator is accepted into a downstream stack.

\subsection{A general condition}
Two results locate where pose could have helped. At $2$\,s, \minADE is
$0.091$\,m against roughly $2.8$\,m of travel: short-horizon forecasting is nearly
\emph{saturated} by constant-velocity extrapolation, leaving almost no error to
remove. At long horizons the residual is dominated by destination and intent,
which body configuration does not disclose. Stratifying instead by how badly
constant-velocity extrapolation fails---the sharpest definition of ``not
saturated''---still yields no effect.

We therefore state the condition in general form: \emph{an auxiliary modality
improves a forecast only where the baseline is neither saturated nor dominated by
intent the modality cannot observe.} Both clauses must hold. For pedestrian
forecasting on WOMD the window is empty, and the same argument predicts failure
for any appearance-derived signal evaluated under these conditions.

\section{What This Means in Practice}
For an AV stack, three consequences follow. First, if the object tracker already
publishes a heading, a pose branch is likely to duplicate it less accurately;
verify against a shuffled-pose control before spending capacity on it. Second, the
capacity cost of an auxiliary branch is real and measurable (${\sim}1.6\%$ here)
and is easily mistaken for a modality effect in either direction---early fusion
avoids it. Third, do not accept a pose estimator on aggregate MPJPE alone: check
per-joint skill, because the joints an aggregate flatters may be the ones carrying
a prior.

\textbf{Limitations.} The largest rung we train is $25$k scenes; the corrected
export covers $299{,}220$, so the full rung is a compute limit rather than an
annotation one. At eight seeds our minimum detectable content effect is
${\approx}1.1\%$. Poses are LiDAR-derived and WOMD ships no raw imagery, so a
camera-based pose or gaze estimate---the one configuration our mechanism suggests
could differ---cannot be tested at this scale. We do not model pose--pose
interaction: cues that live \emph{between} pedestrians rather than within one are
outside what we tested.

\section{Conclusion}
Body pose, recovered from LiDAR, does not improve pedestrian trajectory
forecasting on WOMD. The null is controlled against a shuffled-pose baseline,
holds across scale, representation, integration, a directed search and a
pre-registered confirmation, and has a mechanism: the only predictive cue in body
pose is already supplied, more accurately, by the tracker's heading channel, and
the one cue that could be complementary is not recovered from LiDAR at all. The
practical lesson generalises past pose---an auxiliary modality pays only where the
baseline leaves error to remove and the cause of that error is visible in the new
input.
