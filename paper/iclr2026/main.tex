\begin{abstract}
Body pose is a plausible but rarely used signal for pedestrian trajectory
forecasting in autonomous driving. We extend the Motion Transformer (MTR) with a
GRU encoder over past SMPL body poses (6D rotation representation) and measure
what pose conditioning is actually worth on a pedestrian subset of the Waymo Open
Motion Dataset, using LiDAR-derived pose pseudo-ground-truth.
Our study has three parts. First, a \emph{data-forensics audit}. An earlier draft
of this work reported that pose helps in one configuration. We then found that the
SMPL export keyed pose clips by a scene-\emph{local} object id across all
${\sim}326$k scenes, so nearly every training sequence carried \emph{another
pedestrian's} body motion and $4.7\times$ of the pedestrians were silently
dropped; the correct-pose condition had never been run. A per-scene re-export
(299{,}220 scenes, 1.81M sequences) cut root-heading error from $41.5^\circ$ to
$11.4^\circ$ and raised gait--speed correlation from $r{=}0.26$ to $0.58$.

Second, a \emph{seed-replicated measurement on the corrected data}, in which the
decisive comparison is never pose against no-pose---which confounds information
with branch capacity---but correct pose against the \emph{same architecture fed
another pedestrian's pose}. On that test we find nothing, anywhere: from scratch
$-0.66\%$ ($p{=}0.46$), fine-tuned $-0.88\%$ ($p{=}0.11$), and at $40\times$ more
data $-0.04\%$ ($p{=}0.97$), while the baseline itself improves $19\%$ from data
alone. A directed agentic architecture search over the pose-integration code (two
rounds, 20 scored candidates) accepted none, and its most promising candidate,
which looked significant on a truncated proxy, measured $-0.04\%$ ($p{=}0.92$) at
$n{=}8$ seeds under the full recipe on a held-out split.

Third, a \emph{mechanism}. Body facing is genuinely informative for pedestrians
who are standing still and then step off---but the tracker's box heading already
estimates it, more accurately than mesh fitting recovers it
($25.2^\circ$ vs.\ $44.2^\circ$ median error), and that channel is already an
input to the model; pose adds nothing on top of it in any range, occlusion or
jitter stratum. Per-joint scoring against ground-truth keypoints shows why the
head in particular is unusable: it carries a tenth of the skill of any limb joint
while its MPJPE looks better than either wrist. We report ten self-caught
artifacts, including three of our own headline claims, and conclude with the
condition under which an auxiliary modality can pay at all.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ===========================================================================

Predicting where pedestrians will walk is a safety-critical problem in autonomous
driving. State-of-the-art trajectory forecasting models such as \mtr
\citep{shi2022mtr,shi2023mtrpp} achieve impressive accuracy by operating on
observed agent positions, velocities, and HD map geometry---but they discard a
potentially rich source of information: the \emph{body pose} of the pedestrian.

Human gait encodes intent. A pedestrian who is turning to face a crosswalk will
likely cross it; one whose torso is oriented away from traffic is unlikely to step
into the road. A runner's body orientation predicts turns before the feet complete
them. Modern perception systems increasingly provide body pose estimates
\citep{zhu2022motionbert}. While recent work uses skeleton keypoints for
pose-conditioned prediction in robot navigation and pedestrian benchmarks
\citep{salzmann2023robots,saadatnejad2024socialtransmotion,gao2025socialpose},
state-of-the-art autonomous driving forecasters operating on Waymo
\citep{ettinger2021waymo} discard body pose entirely.
This paper asks: \emph{does conditioning on past SMPL body poses improve
pedestrian trajectory prediction within a SOTA AV forecasting framework,
and if so, what design choices drive the gain?}

We extend \mtr with a \emph{pose conditioning branch} that encodes the past 11
timesteps of SMPL body pose \citep{loper2015smpl} using 6D rotation representations
\citep{zhou2019continuity}, a GRU encoder, and auxiliary future-pose prediction
heads. We train and evaluate on a pedestrian subset of the Waymo Open Motion
Dataset \citep{ettinger2021waymo} paired with LiDAR-derived SMPL pose
pseudo-ground-truth from Waymo-3DSkelMo \citep{zhu2025waymo3dskelmo},
using minADE and minFDE over 6 predicted modes.

This paper deliberately reports both a \emph{result} and a sequence of
\emph{corrections of our own earlier results}, because the corrections are the
instructive part. An earlier single-run draft credited geodesic SO(3) supervision
with $-7.6\%$ \minADE; an audit showed that was impossible---the future pose
targets are absent, so the loss is a constant with identically zero gradient and
the two runs compared were the same experiment under different seeds
(Section~\ref{sec:loss_ablation}). A later draft, rebuilt with five seeds, reported
that pose helps in one configuration. That claim did not survive either: its own
shuffled-pose control reproduced the effect, and we then discovered that the pose
export had been keyed by a scene-local object id, so every pose-conditioned run we
had ever performed trained on another pedestrian's body motion. Everything below
is measured on a corrected per-scene re-export.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{No pose benefit, at any scale or regime, against the control that
        matters.} The decisive comparison is not pose versus no-pose---which
        confounds information with the capacity of an extra input branch---but
        correct pose versus the \emph{same architecture fed a different
        pedestrian's pose}. That test is null from scratch ($-0.66\%$, $p{=}0.46$),
        after full-Waymo pretraining ($-0.88\%$, $p{=}0.11$), and at $40\times$ more
        data ($-0.04\%$, $p{=}0.97$), across six pose representations and both
        branch and early-fusion integrations. Meanwhile the no-pose baseline
        improves $19\%$ from data alone, and the pose \emph{branch} costs a
        consistent ${\sim}1.6\%$ whether or not the poses it receives are the right
        ones.
  \item \textbf{A directed search does not find one either.} We let a coding agent
        rewrite the pose-integration code as the variation operator of an
        evolutionary search over a frozen training repository, with a held-out
        evaluation split the loop never sees. Two rounds and 20 scored candidates
        accepted none. The most promising candidate reached $-1.21\%$ on the
        held-out split under a truncated 12-epoch proxy; at the full 30-epoch recipe
        with $n{=}8$ seeds it measured $-0.04\%$ ($p{=}0.92$)---a convergence-rate
        artifact, and the tenth self-caught artifact in this project.
  \item \textbf{The null has a mechanism, and it generalizes.} Body facing predicts
        which way a standing pedestrian steps off at roughly twice chance---but the
        tracker's box heading estimates the same quantity better
        ($25.2^\circ$ vs.\ $44.2^\circ$ median error), is already an input channel to
        the model, and is not beaten by pose in any range, occlusion or heading-jitter
        stratum. Scoring the pose estimator per joint against ground-truth keypoints
        shows the head carries a tenth of the skill of any limb joint while its MPJPE
        looks \emph{better} than either wrist---so MPJPE rank-orders joints roughly
        backwards with respect to information. We close with the condition an
        auxiliary modality must satisfy to pay at all.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Trajectory Forecasting for Autonomous Driving}

Modern trajectory prediction methods model multi-agent interactions over HD map
features using transformer architectures
\citep{vaswani2017attention,shi2022mtr,shi2023mtrpp,yuan2021agentformer,
salzmann2020trajectron}. Social GAN \citep{gupta2018socialgan} introduced
generative multimodal prediction for pedestrians; \mtr
\citep{shi2022mtr} advances this with global intention localization and
local refinement via cross-attention over map and agent context.
None of these methods incorporate body pose information.

\subsection{Human Pose Estimation and Pose Representations}

SMPL \citep{loper2015smpl} provides a parametric body model where each joint
rotation is represented as a 3D rotation matrix. For neural network learning,
\citet{zhou2019continuity} demonstrate that 6D rotation representations (the
first two columns of $SO(3)$) are continuous and better conditioned than
quaternions or axis-angle, particularly under gradient-based optimization.
The AMASS dataset \citep{mahmood2019amass} provides a large-scale archive of
motion-captured body animations in SMPL parameterization and is widely used to
train human motion priors \citep{rempe2021humor,he2022nemf}.
Waymo-3DSkelMo \citep{zhu2025waymo3dskelmo} combines LiDAR-based human mesh
recovery \citep{fan2023lidarhmr} with such priors to produce temporally coherent
SMPL pose sequences for Waymo pedestrians; we use its output as pose
pseudo-ground-truth.
Works such as MotionBERT \citep{zhu2022motionbert} and HumanMAC
\citep{chen2023humanmac} focus on pose quality but do not connect body pose to
downstream trajectory prediction. A separate line uses 2D pose for pedestrian
\emph{crossing-intention} classification \citep{fang2018pedestrian,rasouli2019pie},
supporting the premise that body configuration carries intent, though without
producing metric trajectory forecasts.

\subsection{Joint Pose and Trajectory Models}

Several recent works condition trajectory prediction on body pose.
\citet{salzmann2023robots} leverage 3D skeletal keypoints from RGB-D cameras
to improve trajectory prediction in robot navigation scenarios.
\citet{saadatnejad2024socialtransmotion} integrate 2D/3D skeleton pose cues
into a transformer-based predictor on pedestrian benchmarks, and
\citet{gao2025socialpose} extend this line to social navigation datasets
using skeleton-based body language features.
In contrast, our work uses parametric SMPL body pose in 6D rotation space---a
richer, anatomically-grounded representation---within the state-of-the-art MTR
framework on Waymo, and, with seed-replicated experiments and per-pedestrian
confidence intervals, isolates which design choices actually drive the gain:
auxiliary pose-loss design, temporal encoding (GRU vs.\ cross-attention $\pm$
positional encoding), future-pose supervision quality, and the interaction of
pose with HD map context and backbone pretraining.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Background: Motion Transformer (MTR)}
\label{sec:mtr}

\mtr \citep{shi2022mtr} is a transformer-based trajectory predictor for the
Waymo Open Motion Dataset. For each \emph{center object} (the agent being predicted),
it encodes a scene context tensor from agent histories and HD map polylines via
a PointNet-like encoder, then decodes $K=6$ trajectory modes through
iterative cross-attention over the scene context, guided by $K$ learned
intention queries that are globally localized to trajectory clusters.

The loss has three components: a mode classification loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}}$,
a winner-takes-all regression loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}$, and a velocity
consistency loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{vel}}$.

\subsection{Pose Conditioning Branch}
\label{sec:pose_branch}

We add a pose conditioning branch alongside the trajectory decoder, illustrated
in Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}. The branch has four components:

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_architecture.pdf}
\caption{%
  \textbf{MTR+Pose architecture.}
  A GRU pose encoder processes the past 11 SMPL body poses (each represented
  as a 144D 6D-rotation vector) and produces a 256D hidden state that is
  concatenated with the MTR agent feature and projected before the decoder.
  Auxiliary future-pose prediction heads are trained with a winner-takes-all
  L1 loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{wta}}$ on 6D rotations; gradients from this
  auxiliary task are the only path by which pose supervision shapes the
  shared features.
  The trajectory decoder and map attention modules of MTR are unchanged.
}
\label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{6D Rotation Encoding.}
Each SMPL body pose at timestep $t$ consists of 24 joint rotations. We represent
each rotation as a 6D vector \citep{zhou2019continuity} (the first two columns of
the $3{\times}3$ rotation matrix), giving a 144D pose vector per timestep.

\paragraph{GRU Pose Encoder.}
Given the past $T=11$ pose vectors $\mathbf{p}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{p}_{T} \in \mathbb{R}^{144}$,
a two-layer GRU with hidden size 256 processes the sequence:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{h}_t = \text{GRU}(\mathbf{p}_t, \mathbf{h}_{t-1}), \quad \mathbf{h}_T \in \mathbb{R}^{256}.
\end{equation}
The final hidden state $\mathbf{h}_T$ is concatenated with the MTR agent feature
and projected to 256D via an MLP, forming the fused agent representation.

\paragraph{Pose Prediction Heads.}
Each of the decoder layers additionally predicts $K$ modes of future poses:
at layer $\ell$ and mode $k$, a linear head outputs 6D rotations for all 80
future timesteps (a $80{\times}144$ output). These heads are auxiliary---their
predictions never feed back into the trajectory pathway; their only effect on
trajectory prediction is through the gradients their losses send into the shared
decoder features and the GRU encoder.

\paragraph{Auxiliary Pose Losses.}
Let $\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{ij} \in \mathbb{R}^6$ be the predicted 6D rotation and
$\mathbf{r}^{ij} \in \mathbb{R}^6$ be the ground-truth 6D rotation for joint $j$
at future step $i$. We consider four auxiliary signals:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Winner-takes-all L1 (WTA-L1)}: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{wta}}$ selects,
        per pedestrian, the pose mode closest to the ground truth in 6D L2 distance,
        and applies an L1 regression loss to that mode, masked by per-timestep
        trajectory validity. (In our configuration files this loss is named
        \texttt{gmm\_pose} for historical reasons; it is a plain WTA regression,
        not a Gaussian mixture likelihood.)

  \item \textbf{Geodesic loss}: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{geo}} = \frac{1}{IJ}\sum_{i,j}
        \arccos\!\left(\text{clamp}\!\left(\frac{\text{tr}(\mathbf{R}_i^j{}^\top \hat{\mathbf{R}}_i^j) - 1}{2},\,-1{+}\epsilon,\,1{-}\epsilon\right)\right)$,
        where $\mathbf{R}, \hat{\mathbf{R}} \in SO(3)$ are rotation matrices
        recovered from the 6D representations of the WTA-selected mode, averaged
        over all future steps (unmasked). The clamp ($\epsilon{=}10^{-7}$) avoids the
        divergent $\arccos$ derivative at the $\pm1$ boundary.

  \item \textbf{MPJPE}: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{mpjpe}} = \frac{1}{IJ}\sum_{i,j}
        \|\mathbf{x}_i^j - \hat{\mathbf{x}}_i^j\|_1$, where $\mathbf{x}$ are joint
        positions computed via forward kinematics on SMPL.

  \item \textbf{Pose mode classification}: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{cls\_pose}}$,
        a cross-entropy loss on per-mode pose scores against the WTA-selected mode.
\end{itemize}

The total loss adds the three MTR motion terms ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}} +
\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}} + 0.5\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{vel}}$) to the weighted pose
terms $w_{\text{wta}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{wta}} + w_{\text{geo}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{geo}}
+ w_{\text{mpjpe}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{mpjpe}} + w_{\text{cls\_pose}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{cls\_pose}}$;
we ablate $w_\star \in \{0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2\}$ (weights of $1.0$ diverge to NaN
through the SMPL forward pass, so all stable runs use $w \leq 0.2$).

\paragraph{A caveat discovered post-hoc: future pose ground truth is absent in the main data condition.}
\label{par:inert}
In our primary (10\,fps) data condition, SMPL pose observations cover only the
\emph{past} window ($t \leq 1.0$\,s); future pose targets are zero for 99.99\% of
timesteps (5 of 91{,}520 in the training split). As we show analytically and
empirically in Section~\ref{sec:loss_ablation}, this renders the geodesic term
\emph{inert} (identically zero gradient against all-zero targets, which map to the
all-zero matrix under Gram--Schmidt), while WTA-L1, MPJPE, and
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{cls\_pose}}$ stay active but pull predictions toward the
degenerate zero targets. We report it prominently because it reverses a conclusion
an earlier draft drew from single-run comparisons.

\subsection{Cross-Attention Pose Encoders (Ablation)}
\label{sec:cross_attn}

To isolate the contribution of temporal ordering from sequential integration, we
test two cross-attention pose encoder variants. Let $\mathbf{q} \in \mathbb{R}^{256}$
be the MTR agent feature (projected query) and $\mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{11 \times 256}$
be the 11 projected pose frames (keys and values). The cross-attention output is:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{c} = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{K}^\top}{\sqrt{256}}\right)\mathbf{V},
\end{equation}
and $\mathbf{c}$ is concatenated with $\mathbf{q}$ and projected to 256D, replacing $\mathbf{h}_T$ in the fusion step.

\textbf{Cross-attention without positional encoding.} No temporal information is injected before attention:
$\mathbf{K} = \{W_K \mathbf{p}_t\}_{t=1}^{11}$, $\mathbf{V} = \{W_V \mathbf{p}_t\}_{t=1}^{11}$.
This treats pose frames as a permutation-invariant set and tests whether the
raw content of each pose frame---absent any ordering---is sufficient to condition
trajectory prediction.

\textbf{Cross-attention with sinusoidal positional encoding.} Standard sinusoidal positional encoding
\citep{vaswani2017attention} is added to the projected keys and values before attention:
$\mathbf{K}' = \{W_K \mathbf{p}_t + \mathbf{e}_t\}_{t=1}^{11}$, where $\mathbf{e}_t$ is
the fixed sinusoidal embedding for frame index $t \in \{0, \ldots, 10\}$.
This injects temporal ordering at zero extra parameter cost and tests whether
ordering alone is sufficient, without the causal accumulation structure of a GRU.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Dataset and Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Dataset.}
We use a pedestrian-only subset of the Waymo Open Motion Dataset
\citep{ettinger2021waymo} paired with SMPL body pose pseudo-ground-truth from the
Waymo-3DSkelMo pipeline \citep{zhu2025waymo3dskelmo}. The subset contains 579
training scenes (1{,}348 pedestrians, of which 1{,}144 have sufficient valid past
observations to serve as prediction targets) and 5{,}171 validation scenes
(8{,}282 pedestrians). Ground-truth future trajectories come from Waymo tracking
data (91 timesteps at 10\,Hz; 9.0\,s total span --- 1.0\,s past plus an 8.0\,s
future horizon). 6{,}688 of the 8{,}282 validation
pedestrians (80.8\%) have valid future trajectory data and enter the evaluation.

\paragraph{Pose provenance.}
The SMPL poses are not motion-capture ground truth: they are estimated from Waymo
LiDAR by the Waymo-3DSkelMo pipeline \citep{zhu2025waymo3dskelmo}, which fits
per-frame SMPL bodies with LiDAR-HMR \citep{fan2023lidarhmr} and refines them with
AMASS-trained motion priors \citep{mahmood2019amass,rempe2021humor,he2022nemf}. Two
properties matter for our results. \emph{First}, pose observations exist only for the
past window in our main condition, so no future information leaks into the inputs; the
evaluation is sound. \emph{Second}, the pipeline snaps the SMPL root rotation to the
past-trajectory motion direction whenever the pedestrian is moving and the raw LiDAR
estimate deviates by more than $90^{\circ}$, so the root-orientation channel is
partially derived from position history the model already receives---an overlap we
quantify in Section~\ref{sec:why}. Body configuration and gait within
$\pm 90^{\circ}$ are genuinely perception-derived.

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
We report \textbf{\minADE} (minimum Average Displacement Error over 6 modes, in meters)
and \textbf{\minFDE} (minimum Final Displacement Error). Lower is better.
The evaluation covers the full 8.0\,s future horizon (80 steps at 10\,Hz).
As an externally interpretable anchor we also compute \textbf{MR@2m} (fraction of
pedestrians with best-checkpoint \minFDE $>2$\,m): $0.239$ no-map, $0.145$ map,
$0.093$ pretrained; pose moves it by $\leq 0.005$ in any cell. Official Waymo
metrics (mAP, gated miss rate) require full retained predictions, which our
per-epoch protocol does not store.

\paragraph{Training and statistical protocol.}
All models train for 30 epochs with batch size 10 on a single RTX 4090
(AdamW, LR $10^{-4}$, step decay $\times 0.5$ at epochs 10/20/25, gradient-norm
clip 1.0); a full run takes 10--15 minutes. Validation is run at epochs
1, 2, 4, every second epoch up to 20, and every epoch from 21 to 30.
Following common practice, our per-run headline number is the \emph{best}
validation \minADE over these evaluations; because best-of-$n$ selection on a
noisy curve inflates apparent differences, every key comparison is additionally
run with \textbf{three to five random seeds} (five for the headline, encoder, map,
and pretrain cells; three for secondary cells), and we report
mean~$\pm$~std across seeds
together with \textbf{paired bootstrap} 95\% confidence intervals over the
6{,}688 evaluated pedestrians (per-pedestrian errors averaged across seeds,
resampled jointly for both models in a pair; 10{,}000 resamples).

\paragraph{Scale caveat.}
The 579-scene training subset is a small fraction of the full Waymo training set
(${\approx}487$k scenarios), so absolute \minADE values in
Sections~\ref{sec:res_core}--\ref{sec:res_hide} are far from
full-data state of the art; relative, seed-replicated comparisons between models
trained on identical data are the object of study.
Section~\ref{sec:res_ladder} quantifies the scale effect directly.

\subsection{Reading the Tables: the Comparison That Matters}
\label{sec:contenttest}

Every number below is computed on the corrected per-scene re-export
(Section~\ref{sec:setup}); nothing from the id-collided export is reused. The
validation set is fixed throughout: $5{,}171$ scenes and $16{,}486$ evaluated
pedestrians. There are three arms. \emph{No-pose} receives trajectories and map
only. \emph{Pose} adds the pedestrian's own SMPL sequence. \emph{Shuffled} is the
identical architecture fed \emph{another pedestrian's} SMPL sequence.

Pose versus no-pose confounds the information in the body signal with the capacity
of an extra input branch; pose versus shuffled holds parameters, optimiser, input
dimensionality and recipe fixed, and isolates content. We call the latter the
\emph{content test} and treat it as decisive. Relative differences are $(A-B)/B$
for ``$A$ vs.\ $B$''; negative means $A$ is better. The primary test is a
hierarchical bootstrap resampling seeds and then pedestrians (hier~$p$); a
pedestrian-only paired bootstrap ignores between-seed variance, is
anti-conservative, and is never used as a gate.

\paragraph{The branch-capacity tax.}
At $25$k both pose arms are significantly worse than no-pose---pose $+1.66\%$
($p{=}0.0001$), shuffled $+1.62\%$ ($p{=}0.0082$)---and indistinguishable from
each other ($+0.04\%$, $p{=}0.97$). The branch costs about $1.6\%$, and it costs
the same whether it carries the right body or the wrong one. A pose-versus-no-pose
comparison at this rung would report a significant \emph{harm} from pose; the
content test shows the harm is architecture, not content.

\paragraph{The tuning phantom.}
The $5$k rung first read as catastrophic. With the learning rate inherited from
the $579$ recipe ($10^{-4}$, one seed) pose was $+37.1\%$ versus no-pose; at
$5{\times}10^{-5}$ for eight epochs, $+7.4\%$; with per-arm selection and full
training, $-0.90\%$, n.s. The $25$k per-arm sweep (four rates $\times$ three seeds
$\times$ three arms) gives every arm an interior optimum at $2.5{\times}10^{-5}$
and moves \minADE by up to $16.7\%$ across the grid, against a content signal of
$0.04$--$0.09\%$: the learning rate is roughly $190\times$ the treatment. A
pose-versus-baseline claim that does not tune each arm separately is a claim about
the learning rate. (The eight-epoch and grid values come from training logs rather
than our archived evaluation outputs.)

\subsection{Representation and Integration}
\label{sec:res_repr}

The null could be a property of how pose enters the model rather than of what it
contains. We tested the three ways that could be so.

\paragraph{Coordinate frame.}
Trajectories are agent-centric, but the SMPL root orientation had remained in the
world frame, so body facing \emph{relative to travel} was not expressible from the
input. The defect touches $6$ of $144$ dimensions of the 6D representation and all
$72$ dimensions of joint positions. After rotating the root into the agent frame,
the median $|\text{body facing} - \text{travel heading}|$ is $12.0^\circ$ for
correct pose against $93.4^\circ$ for shuffled---the first configuration in this
study in which the control is separable from the treatment \emph{at the input}. It
is not separable at the output: the frame-fixed branch ($n{=}3$) scores
$0.3794 \pm 0.0024$ against its own shuffled control $0.3768 \pm 0.0071$, a
content test of $+0.68\%$ (hier~$p{=}0.37$), with $+2.23\%$ versus seed-matched
no-pose ($p{=}0.0006$)---the branch tax again.

\paragraph{Early fusion, no branch.}
To remove the branch tax rather than pay it, we replaced the $144$-d branch with
eight frame-invariant descriptors (facing-vs-motion, torso yaw rate, stride phase,
cadence, arm swing, spine tilt) appended to the agent input channels
($29 \to 37$), so pose enters through the existing encoder. Result:
$0.3711 \pm 0.0051$; $-0.01\%$ versus seed-matched no-pose (hier~$p{=}0.9988$);
$-1.66\%$ versus the $144$-d branch design ($p{=}0.0056$); content test $-0.48\%$
($p{=}0.65$). Pose can be carried for free. It still does not pay.

\paragraph{A directed search over integrations.}
Hand-designed integrations sample a small space. We therefore ran a directed
search in which a coding agent rewrites the pose-integration code as the variation
operator of an evolutionary loop against a frozen, commit-pinned training
repository, scoring on a dev split and never seeing a held-out final split. A
candidate is accepted only if it improves aggregate \minADE by at least $0.8\%$.
Round~1 scored $13$ candidates and accepted none, its best improving \minADE by
$0.49\%$. Round~2 widened the input to $216$ dimensions by adding canonical-frame
joint positions, scored seven and accepted none, its best improving by $0.76\%$,
below the floor. Two module-mode candidates reproduced the branch tax, worsening
\minADE by $1.44\%$ and $4.66\%$.

\subsection{Where a Benefit Could Still Hide}
\label{sec:res_hide}

An aggregate null can hide a benefit local in time, in behaviour, or in a
subpopulation. We looked in the three places a body-pose prior should show first,
on the eight confirmation seeds and the held-out split.

\paragraph{Horizon.}
The content test is $+0.48\%$ at $2$\,s ($p{=}0.32$), $+0.24\%$ at $4$\,s
($p{=}0.52$), $-0.04\%$ at $8$\,s ($p{=}0.92$) and $-0.04\%$ on \minFDE
($p{=}0.95$): null at every horizon and \emph{least} favourable at the shortest.
At $2$\,s \minADE is $0.091$\,m over roughly $2.8$\,m of travel.

\paragraph{Manoeuvre.}
For the $6{,}764$ validation agents with motion covariates we ran the content test
within each quartile and the top decile of four covariates: residual from
constant-velocity extrapolation, heading variation, speed range, and peak
acceleration. Of $20$ tests, zero are significant. The hardest decile by
constant-velocity residual (mean \minADE $1.04$\,m) gives $-0.13\%$, $p{=}0.80$.

\paragraph{Subpopulation.}
Standing-then-moving pedestrians---stationary in the observed history, moving in
the future---are the canonical case for a body-pose prior: the trajectory carries
no velocity and the body is supposed to carry the intent. On the full validation
set they number $1{,}809$. Content test: $+0.38\%$, $p{=}0.80$.

Across Sections~\ref{sec:res_core}--\ref{sec:res_hide} the content test is null in
every configuration, at every training scale, at every horizon, in every manoeuvre
stratum, and in the subpopulation built to favour it. The only effects that
survive the seed count are the branch-capacity tax, in three forms, and one
selection artifact.

\subsection{The Auxiliary-Loss Audit: an Inert Term Credited with a Headline Gain}
\label{sec:loss_ablation}

An earlier draft ablated the pose supervision with one run per configuration and
concluded geodesic-only supervision was the critical ingredient ($0.6231$,
$-7.6\%$ vs.\ baseline $0.6745$), beating ``WTA-L1 only'' ($0.6467$). Three facts
reverse that conclusion.

\paragraph{Fact 1: the geodesic term received zero gradient.}
In the 10\,fps data condition, future pose targets are zero for all but 5 of
91{,}520 training steps (Appendix~\ref{app:dataset}). A zero 6D target maps under
Gram--Schmidt to the all-zero matrix $\mathbf{R}_{\text{gt}}{=}\mathbf{0}$, for
which $\text{tr}(\hat{\mathbf{R}}^\top \mathbf{R}_{\text{gt}}) \equiv 0$ for
\emph{any} prediction, so $\mathcal{L}_{\text{geo}} \equiv \arccos(-\tfrac12)$, a
constant; we verified its gradient is exactly zero (and nonzero under random targets).

\paragraph{Fact 2: the two compared configurations were therefore identical.}
The ``geodesic-only'' run also carried WTA-L1 at $w{=}0.1$, so with the geodesic
term inert it ran the same training distribution as ``WTA-L1 only''---their
$0.0236$ gap ($0.6231$ vs.\ $0.6467$) is pure run-to-run noise, larger than most
effects the draft reported.

\paragraph{Fact 3: on the corrected data the losses are inert \emph{by construction}.}
The re-export stores past-only sequences (at most $11$ frames), so the future-pose
validity mask is empty and all four auxiliary pose losses evaluate to exactly
$0.0$; we confirmed this by instrumenting each term during training. The
consequence is stronger than ``ineffective'': on this data an auxiliary-loss cell
differs from the no-pose baseline only by possessing a pose \emph{encoder}. Any
auxiliary-loss comparison we or others report on WOMD-derived pose of this form is
a comparison of encoders, not of supervision. Table~\ref{tab:loss_ablation}
records the wrong-pose-era measurements that first exposed the noise floor; we
retain it as documentation of the artifact, not as evidence about pose.

from being ``the critical ingredient'', the auxiliary losses are---with the GRU

Four of fifteen runs across the WTA-L1 and MPJPE+WTA-L1 cells converged badly
($\geq 0.71$), whereas the no-auxiliary control produced no such seed in five
runs. At our statistical power the active-loss variants cannot be usefully ranked
against one another, and the earlier draft's mechanistic story about geodesic
gradients shaping the GRU was fitted to noise.

\paragraph{The mask fix tames the worst failures, but adds no signal.} Because the
audit shows the pose losses train against absent targets, we re-ran the audit cells
(five seeds each) with a corrected future-pose validity mask (losses averaged only
over the $<{0.01\%}$ of steps that carry a real target; this is the released code).
Masking removes the WTA-specific catastrophic tail---the masked WTA-L1 cell's worst

leaves the no-pose baseline unchanged ($0.6605 \pm 0.0153$ masked vs.\ $0.6604$;
$p{=}0.90$). The on-10fps auxiliary pose signal is, as predicted, inert; the no-map
regime is noisy with or without it.

\emph{pre-fix} code (tagged in the release); the released code masks the future-pose
losses, so re-running any aux-active cell yields the masked training distribution.
The baseline is verified unchanged (paired $p{=}0.90$, hierarchical $p{=}0.99$) and the four audit cells are re-run
masked above; other aux-active cells were not, and may differ under the fix.

\subsection{Pose Quality: Which Joints LiDAR-based HMR Actually Recovers}
\label{sec:recoverability}

A null result invites the objection that the input pose was simply too poor to
help. Aggregate MPJPE answers that objection badly, because it cannot separate two
very different situations: an estimator that tracks a joint imprecisely, and an
estimator that ignores the input and emits its \emph{prior}---roughly the mean
human pose. The second is what breaks downstream consumers, and it is invisible in
MPJPE, since a joint that barely moves scores well merely by predicting the
population mean.

We therefore score each joint against the strongest trivial competitor: always
predicting the mean root-aligned ground-truth pose. With
$\text{MPJPE}_j$ the error at joint $j$ and $\text{MP}_j$ the error of that
constant predictor,
\[
  \mathrm{SKILL}_j \;=\; 1 - \text{MPJPE}_j / \text{MP}_j ,
\]
so $\mathrm{SKILL}_j>0$ means the estimator beats the mean pose and
$\mathrm{SKILL}_j\le 0$ means it contributes nothing at that joint. We evaluate the
LiDAR-HMR stage against ground-truth LiDAR keypoints on the Waymo Perception
validation split, matching frames by timestamp, over the 13 joints common to both
skeletons (Table~\ref{tab:recoverability}).

$46$--$64\%$. The head does not: it cuts error by $4.5\%$, an order of magnitude

$0.70$--$1.10$ for the limbs---shows the estimator additionally under-dispersing
it. The head's skill is nonetheless significantly positive
($[+.022,+.067]$): the model is not entirely ignoring it, and we make the
quantitative claim rather than a null one. (At a fifth of this sample the head's CI
did span zero; that CI was a power artifact, and we note it because it is the kind
of small-$n$ null this paper elsewhere cautions against.)

The consequence for practice is that \textbf{MPJPE rank-orders these joints almost
backwards with respect to information content}. By MPJPE the head ($111.6$\,mm)
looks better than either ankle ($114.0$/$116.6$\,mm) and better than either wrist
($118.3$/$125.4$\,mm), while carrying roughly a tenth of their skill. The head's
flattering error reflects how little the estimator improves on the mean there: its
MPJPE ($111.6$\,mm) sits just below its mean-pose baseline ($116.9$\,mm), whereas
the hips---which have an even smaller ground-truth spread ($97.9$\,mm)---are
predicted at $39.8$\,mm against a $97.9$\,mm baseline. Small spread alone is
therefore not the explanation; what distinguishes the head is that the estimator
does not beat the mean despite it. The physical reading is
straightforward---a pedestrian at $20$--$40$\,m returns a few dozen LiDAR points,
and the head is a near-spherical handful of them with little geometry to constrain
its orientation, so the fit falls back largely on the prior.

This bounds what pose conditioning could have delivered, and it does so
constructively: gait-bearing joints are genuinely measured, so the null in
Section~\ref{sec:res_core} is not an artifact of unusable input; but any
downstream method depending on head or gaze orientation from LiDAR HMR is reading
something close to a prior, whatever the reported MPJPE. We recommend reporting per-joint skill
alongside MPJPE for this reason.

\section{Why pose does not pay}
\label{sec:why}
% ===========================================================================

The null is not a failure of the estimator or of the architecture search. It
follows from what pose measures and what forecasting needs.

\paragraph{Pose carries exactly one predictive cue.}
If body configuration predicts anything a trajectory does not, it is which way a
standing pedestrian will step off. We isolated this as a task. From the corrected
export we took every pedestrian stationary at the prediction time
($\text{speed}<0.3$\,m/s): $224{,}946$ in training and $19{,}939$ in validation,
$99.4\%$ with a fitted pose.\footnote{Deduplicated by (scene, object): Waymo writes
several sample files per scene carrying identical tracks, so the raw extraction is
inflated $5.4\times$.} Onset---a displacement above $1$\,m---follows within $3$\,s
for $8.5\%$ of them. On the $1{,}705$ positive events at the $3$\,s horizon, SMPL
body facing predicts onset direction at roughly twice chance: median angular error
$44.2^\circ$ and $50.6\%$ within $45^\circ$, against $90^\circ$ and $25\%$ for a
uniform guess.

\paragraph{The tracker already supplies that cue, more accurately.}
Waymo's per-object box heading estimates the same quantity with median error
$25.2^\circ$ and $63.9\%$ within $45^\circ$. Paired on the same events, SMPL is
$7.3^\circ$ worse in median (Wilcoxon $p{=}2.6\times10^{-43}$) and is the better
estimate on only $35\%$ of events---even though the SMPL forward axis was
calibrated \emph{against} the box heading before scoring, a choice that favours
pose. That heading enters the agent encoder as the sine and cosine of the heading
channel. Every pose experiment in this paper therefore competed against a channel
the model already had, in a more accurate form than mesh fitting recovers it.

\paragraph{Pose does not take over where the tracker degrades.}
Stratifying the same paired comparison by range to ego, by history validity (an
occlusion proxy) and by box-heading jitter, the pose-minus-box gap is positive in
all twelve strata (every $p<10^{-5}$) and \emph{widens} as perception degrades:
$+12.3^\circ$ at $2$--$17$\,m rising to $+32.9^\circ$ at $41$--$75$\,m, and
$+16.9^\circ$ on complete tracks rising to $+33.7^\circ$ on gappy ones. In the
jitteriest heading quartile, where the box heading is genuinely poor
($50.5^\circ$), SMPL is worse still ($63.8^\circ$). Both estimates derive from the
same LiDAR returns; they fail together, not complementarily.

\paragraph{And nothing on top of it.}
A model predicting onset direction from box heading alone reaches a median of
$24.8^\circ$ ($18{,}950$ positive training events, $1{,}688$ test). Appending a
full pose descriptor gives $26.5^\circ$ (Wilcoxon $p{=}0.085$ vs.\ box-only);
appending a \emph{shuffled} descriptor gives $25.8^\circ$ ($p{=}0.214$). The small
degradation is the cost of extra feature dimensions, not a property of pose
content. The companion question---whether the pedestrian moves at all---is carried
by kinematics: residual micro-motion alone gives test AUC $0.741$ / $0.703$ /
$0.638$ at $2$ / $3$ / $5$\,s, and pose adds nothing the shuffled control does not
add equally.

\paragraph{The one cue that could have been complementary is not measured.}
Gaze precedes locomotion in human motor control, so head yaw relative to the torso
is a plausible cue independent of the box heading. It is not available from this
sensor. In the fitted meshes, head-versus-torso yaw has a standard deviation of
$3.8^\circ$ (5th--95th percentile $[-4.4^\circ, +5.8^\circ]$), and the head
axis-angle varies about six times less than the knee: the estimator is emitting
its pose prior. The real quantity is far wider---against ground-truth 3D keypoints
on Waymo Perception ($n{=}8{,}234$) its circular standard deviation is
$19.2^\circ$, with $25\%$ of pedestrians beyond $15^\circ$. This is a measurement
failure, not evidence that gaze is behaviourally irrelevant: a pedestrian at
$20$--$40$\,m returns a few dozen LiDAR points, and the head is a near-spherical
handful of them with little geometry to constrain yaw. The ceiling is low even for
an oracle---the residual head yaw would have to explain has a spread of
$66.9^\circ$, so a perfect measurement at $19.2^\circ$ covers under a third of it,
and the fitted value explains $0.11\%$ of it ($r{=}-0.033$, $p{=}0.17$, wrong
sign).

None of this is visible in an aggregate pose error, which is why
Section~\ref{sec:recoverability} scores recoverability per joint: a single MPJPE
cannot distinguish an estimator that tracks a joint imprecisely from one that
ignores it and emits its prior, since both produce a moderate mean distance.

\paragraph{The general condition.}
Two results locate where pose could have helped. Across horizons the content test
is null everywhere and least favourable at the \emph{shortest}: $+0.48\%$ at
$2$\,s against $-0.04\%$ at $8$\,s. At $2$\,s \minADE\ is $0.091$\,m while a
pedestrian at $1.4$\,m\,s$^{-1}$ travels ${\approx}2.8$\,m, so short-horizon
forecasting is very nearly \emph{saturated} by constant-velocity extrapolation and
has almost no error left for a second modality to remove; at long horizons the
residual is dominated by destination and intent, which body configuration does not
disclose. Pose is squeezed out at both ends. Stratifying instead by how badly
constant-velocity extrapolation fails---the sharpest operational definition of
``not saturated''---gives $20$ content tests and zero significant results,
including the hardest residual decile ($-0.13\%$, $p{=}0.80$).

That second result is the decisive one: it removes the saturation objection and the
null survives. We therefore state the principle in the general, falsifiable form
that is the most portable thing this paper can offer. \emph{An auxiliary modality
improves a forecast only where the baseline is neither saturated nor dominated by
intent the modality cannot observe: there must be error left to remove, and its
cause must be legible in the new input.} The two clauses are independent, and the
manoeuvre strata relax only the first---when a pedestrian departs from constant
velocity the cause is typically external (another agent, an obstacle, a kerb, a
signal, a destination) rather than written in their own body. The same argument
predicts failure for any appearance-derived signal under these conditions, and
says where to look instead: regimes where prediction is hard \emph{for reasons
visible in the agent itself}.

\section{Methodology: ten self-caught artifacts}
\label{sec:methodology}
% ===========================================================================

Every quantitative claim this project made about pose, over eighteen months, was
eventually retracted by one of its own checks. We list the mechanisms rather than
the incidents, because the mechanisms transfer.

\paragraph{How an inert loss became a headline result.}
The failure chain is worth spelling out because none of its links is exotic: a
data condition silently degenerated (all-zero future-pose targets), the loss
evaluated to a smooth constant with \emph{zero} gradient rather than crashing, two
functionally identical configurations were each run once, and the resulting
$0.024$ seed gap was read as a $3.8\%$ design effect and given a mechanistic
story. The audit that caught it generalises to two cheap checks: verify the
\emph{target coverage} of every auxiliary loss against the data, and check the
\emph{gradient} of every loss term in isolation on a real batch.

\paragraph{What caught the rest.}
\emph{Seed replication} dissolved four apparent effects that were tight at
$n{\leq}3$: a $-7.6\%$ geodesic gain between two identical configurations, a
$-3.5\%$ ``stable encoder'' win whose next two seeds both failed, a
positional-encoding ordering effect, and---after the data fix---both a
``pose harms'' and a ``shuffled is best'' reading of the same triad.
\emph{Wrong-input controls} were decisive twice: a shuffled-pose arm reproduced
the one surviving pose benefit, and a wrong-pose control turned a significant
intent-classification gain into a null. \emph{Data forensics} produced the largest
correction of all---the export keyed pose clips by a scene-local object id, so
every pose-conditioned experiment we had run to that point used another
pedestrian's body motion. \emph{Protocol discipline} caught three more: a
last-epoch checkpoint convention that manufactured a $+13.4\%$ effect
($p{=}0.0002$) which became $+4.1\%$ ($p{=}0.37$) at best epoch; an inherited
learning rate that made pose look catastrophic at a new scale ($+37\%$, falling
to $-0.9\%$ n.s.\ once each arm was tuned); and a frame-indexing bug in our own
evaluation harness that inflated MPJPE from $87.6$ to $160.6$\,mm.
\emph{Pre-registration with a held-out split} caught the last: a candidate that
reached $-1.21\%$ on data the search never saw, under a truncated proxy recipe,
measured $-0.04\%$ ($p{=}0.92$) at the full recipe---a convergence-rate artifact.

\paragraph{Two statistical lessons.}
First, a percentile bootstrap over per-seed deltas is badly anti-conservative when
the deltas share a sign: at $n{=}5$ it returns $p\approx1/N_{\text{boot}}$
regardless of effect size, and it had manufactured several of the retractions
above. We gate on a paired $t$-test and keep the bootstrap interval as a
descriptive spread. Second, best-checkpoint selection is not neutral between
architectures: it is worth $-1.87\%$ to a wide-input arm and $-0.55\%$ to the
baseline, enough to flip the sign of a cross-architecture comparison. Only the
content test---structurally identical arms differing in whether the input is the
right pedestrian's---is robust to it, which is the deeper reason it is the
comparison we rely on.

At sub-$1\%$ effect sizes, everything is a treatment: the learning rate moves
\minADE\ by up to $16.7\%$, the checkpoint rule by ${\sim}1.9\%$, and the seed by
more than either, against a content signal of $0.04\%$.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
% ===========================================================================

\textbf{Scale.} The largest rung we train is 25{,}000 scenes (142{,}807
pedestrians). The corrected export covers 299{,}220 training scenes, so the full
rung is a compute limitation rather than an annotation one; we state this because
an earlier draft of this paper wrongly claimed pose-conditioning at scale was
infeasible with current annotations. At $n{=}8$ seeds our minimum detectable
effect on the content test is ${\approx}1.1\%$, so effects below that remain
outside our resolution.

\textbf{Pose provenance.} The SMPL pseudo-ground-truth is a pipeline output, not
ground truth. We characterise it rather than assume it: root-aligned MPJPE is
$87.6$\,mm at the mesh-recovery stage and $92.7$\,mm after the motion-prior
stages that the predictor actually consumes, with the degradation concentrated in
the gait joints (knees $+19$\,mm, ankles $+17$/$+25$\,mm); an RGB alternative is
worse ($106.2$\,mm). Section~\ref{sec:recoverability} scores recoverability per
joint. Deployed online pose estimation would carry different noise.

\textbf{Single dataset and modality.} All results are Waymo, and the poses are
LiDAR-derived; WOMD ships no raw imagery, so a camera-based pose or gaze estimate
cannot be tested at this scale. We also do not model pose--pose interaction: each
agent's pose is summarised independently, so social cues that live between
pedestrians rather than within one are outside what we tested.

\textbf{Statistics.} Our primary test is a hierarchical bootstrap resampling seeds
then pedestrians; a pedestrian-only paired bootstrap on seed-averaged errors is
anti-conservative and we do not use it as a gate. For the pre-registered
confirmation the gate is a paired $t$-test over seeds, because a percentile
bootstrap over $n{=}8$ same-signed deltas returns $p\approx1/N_{\text{boot}}$
irrespective of effect size---a failure mode that had previously manufactured
results in this project. We test a large family of pairs without correction; the
trajectory-only effects (map, pretraining) survive any reasonable correction,
and no pose effect is close. Best-checkpoint selection systematically favours
wider-input arms (Section~\ref{sec:confirmation}), which is why cross-architecture
comparisons are reported at a fixed epoch and the content test---structurally
identical arms---is the one we rely on.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ===========================================================================

We have presented \mtrpose, a pose-conditioned extension of the Motion
Transformer over past SMPL body poses, and used it to ask what body pose is worth
for pedestrian trajectory forecasting. The answer is nothing measurable, and the
value of the paper is in how tightly that answer is bounded and why it holds.

Our conclusions. (1) \textbf{The null is robust and controlled.} On a corrected
pose export, correct pose is statistically indistinguishable from the same
architecture fed another pedestrian's pose---from scratch ($-0.66\%$, $p{=}0.46$),
after full-Waymo pretraining ($-0.88\%$, $p{=}0.11$), and at $40\times$ the data
($-0.04\%$, $p{=}0.97$)---across six representations, branch and early-fusion
integrations, a directed agentic search over the integration code, and a
pre-registered $n{=}8$ confirmation on a held-out split ($-0.04\%$, $p{=}0.92$).
The pose \emph{branch} costs ${\sim}1.6\%$ whether or not its input is the right
pedestrian's; early fusion removes that cost but adds no benefit, so pose can be
carried for free and still does not pay.

(2) \textbf{The null has a mechanism.} The one predictive cue in body
pose---facing, for pedestrians about to step off---is already supplied to the
model by the tracker's heading channel, more accurately than mesh fitting recovers
it ($25.2^\circ$ vs.\ $44.2^\circ$), in every range, occlusion and jitter stratum,
and with nothing added on top. Head orientation, the one cue that could have been
complementary, is not recovered from LiDAR at all: fitted head-versus-torso yaw
varies $3.8^\circ$ against $19.2^\circ$ in ground truth.

(3) \textbf{Aggregate pose-quality metrics hide this.} Scored per joint against a
mean-pose baseline, the head carries a tenth of the skill of any limb joint while
its MPJPE looks better than either wrist; MPJPE rank-orders joints roughly
backwards with respect to information content. We recommend reporting per-joint
skill alongside it.

(4) \textbf{An auxiliary modality can only pay where the baseline is neither
saturated nor dominated by unobservable intent.} For pedestrian forecasting on
Waymo that window is empty, at both ends and in every manoeuvre stratum we tested.
The prediction extends to any appearance-derived signal evaluated under the same
conditions.

The methodology, not the pose numbers, is our intended lasting contribution: ten
self-caught artifacts, three of them our own successive headline claims, caught by
seed replication, wrong-input controls, pre-registration, held-out splits,
fixed-epoch endpoints and data forensics. At sub-1\% effect sizes, everything is a
treatment.

\subsection{Architecture Hyperparameters}
\begin{itemize}
  \item GRU pose encoder: GRU(input=144, hidden=256, layers=2, dropout=0.1);
        ${\approx}505$K parameters total including fusion MLP
  \item Cross-attention pose encoder: pose projection 144$\to$256,
        MultiheadAttention(embed\_dim=256, num\_heads=8, dropout=0.1,
        batch\_first=True); ${\approx}497$K parameters total including fusion MLP
  \item Pose input dimension: 144 (24 joints $\times$ 6D rotation)
  \item Past pose window: 11 timesteps (1.0 second at 10\,Hz)
  \item Number of predicted trajectory modes: $K=6$
  \item Number of future steps: 80 (8.0 seconds)
  \item MTR context encoder: 6 local-attention transformer layers, 256 hidden dim,
        8 heads, attention dropout 0.1
  \item MTR decoder: 6 layers, hidden dim 512 (map branch 256), 8 heads
  \item Batch size: 10
  \item Optimizer: AdamW, $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.999$, weight decay 0.01
  \item LR schedule: initial LR $1{\times}10^{-4}$, step decay $\times 0.5$ at
        epochs 10, 20, 25
  \item Epochs: 30
  \item Gradient clipping: norm $\leq 1.0$
  \item Random seeds for replicated runs: 101, 202, 303 (plus 404, 505 for headline cells)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Dataset Construction}
\label{app:dataset}
Waymo agent future trajectories are stored in \texttt{processed\_scenarios/}
PKL files with shape \texttt{(num\_agents, 91, 10)}. SMPL pedestrian files are
matched to Waymo object IDs via integer stem matching. 6{,}688 of 8{,}282
validation pedestrians (80.8\%) have a valid future-trajectory match and are
evaluated; the remainder are excluded. The 91-step grid spans $[0, 9.0]$
seconds; SMPL timestamps (originally in microseconds) are divided by $10^6$
before alignment. In the 10\,fps condition the SMPL observations cover only
$[0, 1.0]$\,s (the past window): across the entire training split only 5 of
91{,}520 future pose-target steps are nonzero, which is the basis for the
inert-geodesic-gradient analysis in Section~\ref{sec:loss_ablation}.

\subsection{Loss Weight Stability}
At $w=1.0$, gradient explosion through the SMPL forward kinematics causes NaN
divergence at epoch 3. The geodesic loss gradient norm was also found to diverge
at the $\pm 1$ boundary of the $\arccos$ argument; we clamp to $[-1+\epsilon, 1-\epsilon]$
with $\epsilon=10^{-7}$. At $w=0.1$, training is stable for all 30 epochs across
all ablation variants.

The earlier draft swept what it believed was the geodesic weight and found a
``sharp optimum'' at $w{=}0.1$: $w{=}0.05$ gave $0.6786$ and $w{=}0.2$ gave

($0.02$--$0.06$) overlap the seed-noise scale measured in
Section~\ref{sec:loss_ablation}, so the curvature of this one-seed sweep should
not be read as a calibrated sensitivity estimate. What survives: $w{=}0.1$ is a
reasonable default, and we did not find evidence that performance is improved by
halving or doubling it.


\subsection{Ablation: Future Pose Supervision Quality}
\label{sec:future_ablation}

To localize the source of improvement, we compare two data conditions, both with
the full auxiliary loss set at $w{=}0.1$, \textbf{seed-replicated (five seeds each)}:
(a) \emph{no future poses} --- future-pose GT is zero, only past poses are observed
(the inert-loss condition of our other ablations): \minADE $0.6618 \pm 0.0366$; and
(b) \emph{30\,fps motion-prior-completed future poses} --- the Waymo-3DSkelMo pipeline
infills 99\% of future timesteps as pseudo-GT, the one setting in which the geodesic
and MPJPE terms receive real, non-degenerate gradients: \minADE $0.6586 \pm 0.0292$.

Activating the future-pose losses changes \minADE by $-0.5\%$ (hierarchical
bootstrap $p{=}0.60$; vs.\ the no-pose baseline $-0.3\%$, $p{=}0.82$):
\textbf{not significant}. Giving the rotation-space losses genuine gradients does
not reliably improve trajectory accuracy over the inert condition --- consistent
with our single-run pilot ($0.6567$ vs.\ $0.6532$), and both conditions carry the
same heavy-tailed seeds as every other no-map cell. Whatever benefit pose
conditioning provides comes from the past-pose encoder, not the future-pose
prediction task.
