\begin{abstract}
Gait and facing appear to encode pedestrian intent that a position history
lacks, making body pose an attractive forecasting input. On the Waymo Open
Motion Dataset, with SMPL poses recovered from LiDAR, we find no measurable
benefit. The decisive comparison is not pose against no pose, which confounds
body information with an extra branch's capacity, but correct pose against the
same architecture fed \emph{another pedestrian's} pose. At our largest scale it
returns $+0.04\%$ ($p{=}0.93$), and the data bound the content effect to
$\pm1.7\%$.

An oracle control rules out a dead pathway: the same branch fed the agent's own
future improves \minADE by $34.8\%$. Corrupting that oracle with controlled
angular error shows what the pathway converts. The gain stays large at every
accuracy we trained, reaching $-13.6\%$ even at $15.1^\circ$ median bearing
error, worse at every percentile than the box heading the model \emph{already}
receives. What the oracle sells is knowledge of which future occurred, and no
sensor supplies that. Body facing is instead a noisier copy of that heading
($12.3^\circ$ against $3.9^\circ$), worst where it would be non-redundant:
$41.2^\circ$ for pedestrians stationary at prediction time. Before integrating a
modality, measure how much of the cue it must carry it explains beyond the
inputs the model already has.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Predicting where a pedestrian will go is a safety-critical function of an
automated vehicle, and body pose is an obvious candidate input. A person's stance,
gait phase and facing direction plausibly reveal intent before their position
changes, and modern perception stacks already produce human mesh or keypoint
estimates. Several recent systems condition trajectory forecasts on
pose~\cite{saadatnejad2024socialtransmotion,gao2025socialpose,salzmann2023robots,kress2022pose}.

This paper reports that, on the Waymo Open Motion Dataset (WOMD), pose
conditioning does not measurably improve pedestrian trajectory accuracy. More
usefully for practitioners, it also identifies a specific reason that can be
checked quantitatively, and cheaply, before a modality is integrated at all.

We make three contributions.

\textbf{A controlled null.} We measure pose conditioning against a shuffled-pose
control: the same architecture receiving a different pedestrian's body motion.
This isolates information content from the capacity cost of an extra branch, and
it is the comparison a practitioner should demand before adopting any auxiliary
modality. The control is null at three training scales; across the three pose
encodings we run it against on the corrected export ($6$D joint rotations,
agent-frame root, and eight frame-invariant descriptors), with constant-pose as
a capacity control; and under two integration styles. Two
further encodings (3D joint positions and a root-orientation-free variant) and a
GRU encoder predate the export fix of Sec.~\ref{sec:setup} and were run against
no-pose rather than against a stranger's pose, so we neither count them as
controlled nulls nor quote numbers from them. An oracle arm, which
replaces the body with a signal encoding the agent's own future, improves
\minADE by $34.8\%$ through the same branch, so the null is a property of pose,
not of the plumbing.

\textbf{What the pathway converts, and what it does not.} We corrupt that oracle
with a controlled angular error to ask what kind of information the branch turns
into accuracy. Every accuracy we trained converts, including one uniformly worse
than an input the model already has, so the ladder does not set an accuracy a
cue must reach; it separates a precision term with a $3.0^\circ$ half-life from a
slowly decaying term that is future-side mode selection. Body facing
supplies neither: it is a noisier copy of the box heading where pedestrians move,
and reaches $41.2^\circ$ where they do not, which is exactly the case velocity
cannot cover.
We also show the same ladder run at $579$ scenes reports a clean accuracy
threshold that $25$k scenes removes, so an underpowered ladder manufactures a
floor where there is none.

\textbf{A measurement caveat.} Aggregate MPJPE cannot distinguish a pose
estimator that tracks a joint imprecisely from one that ignores the input and
emits its prior. Scoring each joint against an always-predict-the-mean-pose
baseline, MPJPE is uninformative about which joints are actually recovered
($\rho{=}-0.11$ across joints, n.s.) and inverts the ordering for the head.
Accepting a pose estimator into an AV stack on a single error number risks
importing a prior.

We also report, as a cautionary note, that our first attempt at this study was
invalidated by an export bug that silently substituted other pedestrians'
bodies, a failure any pose pipeline keyed by object identity can reproduce.

\section{Related Work}
Trajectory forecasting on WOMD is dominated by anchor- and query-based
transformers; we build on MTR~\cite{shi2022mtr,shi2023mtrpp}, which is
representative and strong. Human mesh recovery from LiDAR is
maturing~\cite{fan2023lidarhmr}, with motion priors used to complete sparse
observations~\cite{rempe2021humor,he2022nemf,mahmood2019amass}. Intent-focused
work on pedestrian datasets~\cite{rasouli2019pie,fang2018pedestrian} targets
crossing decisions rather than metric trajectories. Kress
et al.~\cite{kress2022pose} report pose-based gains for vulnerable road users
from a research vehicle, at close range and with camera-derived pose.
Methodologically our closest precedent is Makansi
et al.~\cite{makansi2022walkalone}, who find by feature attribution that
trajectory predictors draw almost nothing from an input the field had assumed
was central.

\textbf{Relation to prior positive results.} Pose conditioning has reported real
gains; our mechanism predicts where they come from rather than contradicting them. Social-Transmotion~\cite{saadatnejad2024socialtransmotion} lowers ADE on
the synthetic JTA dataset from $0.99$ to $0.89$ with \emph{ground-truth} 3D pose,
and, in the harder case for us, from $0.40$ to $0.36$ on JRDB with
\emph{estimated} pose. Social-Pose~\cite{gao2025socialpose} reports about $25\%$
on JTA ($1.20{\to}0.90$) and on JRDB. Salzmann et al.~\cite{salzmann2023robots}
report ${\approx}10\%$ from onboard-estimated keypoints at ranges up to $7$\,m,
specifically for agents with little position history, which is also our best
hypothesis.

Le et al.~\cite{le2026legsoverarms} reach the opposite conclusion on JRDB and, in
doing so, say the most useful thing about ours: they localise the gain to
\emph{lower-body} 3D keypoints ($13\%$ ADE, a further $1$--$4\%$ from derived
biomechanical cues). Their cue is our weakest channel. Of the twelve limb joints
in Table~\ref{tab:skill} the knees and ankles that carry gait sit at the bottom
of the range (\textsc{skill} $+0.46$ to $+0.49$, against $+0.63$ and $+0.64$ for
the elbows), and what leg motion we do recover is damped: predicted knee and
ankle spread is $84$--$91\%$ of ground truth, where elbows and wrists reach
$96$--$99\%$. A panoramic camera at a few metres resolves a swinging leg; a few
dozen LiDAR returns at $40$\,m recover its mean position and lose much of its
amplitude. So the two results do not conflict: theirs is what this cue is worth when the
limbs are resolved and the predictor has no heading input, ours what it is worth
when the trajectory already carries the body's direction. Sparsity alone does not
account for the difference, since our content test is null even on the
best-resolved agents (Sec.~\ref{sec:hide}).

The most informative comparison is within Social-Pose itself. Alongside JTA it
evaluates on Pedestrians and Cyclists in Road Traffic, the one vehicle-captured
dataset there, and, with \emph{ground-truth} 3D pose, ADE moves from $0.58$ to
$0.57$\,m, by $0.01$\,m or less for all five architectures tested, against the
${\approx}25\%$ the same encoder delivers on JTA. The authors attribute this to the shorter horizon and the absence of
interacting neighbours; our measurements suggest an additional reason that
travels with the sensor rather than the horizon. Either way, the closest
published setting to ours already reports approximately our result.

Two properties separate the remaining positive settings from an AV stack, and
both follow from the mechanism in Sec.~\ref{sec:why}. \emph{Range and sensor:}
JRDB is an indoor and campus robot dataset in which pedestrians are metres away
and pose is camera-derived, whereas WOMD pedestrians are observed from $2$ to
$75$\,m and often return a few dozen LiDAR points. \emph{The baseline:} those
predictors consume positions, not a tracker's box heading, so body facing is new
information there and partly duplicated here. On controls, none reports a
shuffled-pose arm, though the confound differs by design. Social-Transmotion
masks modalities within one network, so no extra parameters are involved there,
whereas a dedicated pose branch pays the capacity cost we measure ($+1.7\%$
\minADE), the same order as the smaller reported gains.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Data and a data-integrity failure}
We use WOMD~\cite{ettinger2021waymo} with SMPL~\cite{loper2015smpl} poses
recovered from LiDAR by the Waymo-3DSkelMo pipeline~\cite{zhu2025waymo3dskelmo},
which fits per-frame bodies with LiDAR-HMR~\cite{fan2023lidarhmr} and completes
them using AMASS-trained motion
priors~\cite{mahmood2019amass,rempe2021humor,he2022nemf}. Poses cover the $11$
observed frames; the prediction horizon is $8$\,s at $10$\,Hz. The branch is not
starved: every predicted agent carries at least one valid pose frame, and
$86.7\%$ of observed frames carry one, so sparsity of pose is not an explanation
for anything that follows.

One property of that pipeline bears on Sec.~\ref{sec:why}: when a pedestrian is
moving and the raw LiDAR estimate of root rotation deviates from the
past-trajectory direction by more than $90^\circ$, the pipeline snaps the root to
the direction of travel. Body facing is therefore partly trajectory-derived for
moving pedestrians. This reinforces the redundancy we report rather than
weakening it, and it does not reach the stationary-onset analysis, whose events
are all below $0.3$\,m/s.

Our first pass at this study was invalid. The export keyed pose clips by a
scene-\emph{local} object identifier, so clips collided across scenes and nearly
every training sequence carried another pedestrian's body. A per-scene re-export
($299{,}220$ scenes, $1.81$M sequences) cut the median error between root heading
and travel direction from $41.5^\circ$ to $11.4^\circ$ and raised the
gait--speed correlation from $r{=}0.26$ to $0.58$. We report it because the
failure is silent (training converges, losses look normal) and identity-keyed
joins are common in pose pipelines. Every number here is from the corrected
export.

\subsection{Arms and the content test}
Three arms share one architecture (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). \emph{No-pose} receives trajectories and map.
\emph{Pose} adds the pedestrian's own SMPL sequence. \emph{Shuffled} receives
another pedestrian's sequence, with identical parameter count, optimiser and
input dimensionality. A fourth arm, \emph{oracle}, replaces the SMPL features with a six-channel
encoding of the agent's own future displacement at $3$ and $8$\,s, leaving the
branch and its masks untouched; it shows the pathway can carry information at
all (Sec.~\ref{sec:poscontrol}).

Pose versus no-pose confounds information with branch capacity; pose versus
shuffled isolates content. We call the latter the \emph{content test} and treat it
as decisive. Relative differences are $(A-B)/B$; negative favours $A$.

Inference is over \emph{seeds}, which is the unit the treatment is applied to:
every comparison between trained arms is a paired $t$-test on per-seed
differences. The two exceptions are flagged where they occur: per-pedestrian
subpopulation tests and the per-joint intervals of Table~\ref{tab:skill}, which
resample pedestrians. We also report a hierarchical bootstrap that resamples seeds and
then pedestrians, and it agrees; we do not quote a percentile bootstrap
$p$-value, because with five seeds whose differences share a sign it returns the
resolution floor $1/N_{\text{boot}}$ rather than a calibrated tail probability,
and a pedestrian-only bootstrap ignores between-seed variance entirely.

\subsection{Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
Every arm trains for $30$ epochs with AdamW (batch $2$ per GPU, weight decay
$0.01$, gradient-norm clipping $1.0$) under a step schedule that halves the
learning rate at epochs $10$, $20$ and $25$; the decoder predicts $K{=}6$ modes.
The learning rate is chosen per rung from a grid run separately for each arm:
$1{\times}10^{-4}$ at $579$ scenes, $5{\times}10^{-5}$ at $5$k and
$2.5{\times}10^{-5}$ at $25$k, where every arm peaks at the same interior
optimum. Validation is fixed at $5{,}171$ scenes and $16{,}486$ pedestrians. Headline
numbers are taken at each run's best validation epoch
over a fixed evaluation schedule, and Sec.~\ref{sec:real} reports what changes
under a fixed-epoch rule. Official WOMD mAP and gated miss rate need the full
retained prediction set, which our per-epoch evaluation does not store, so we
report \minADE, \minFDE and miss rate at $2$\,m. Code, configs, per-seed metrics
and the analysis scripts are released with the paper.

\subsection{The null, and its bound}
Table~\ref{tab:ladder} trains all three arms at three scales. Data alone improves
the baseline by $19.2\%$; the content test is null at every rung, in \minADE and
in \minFDE alike, and in miss rate at $2$\,m, where the content test is
$+2.3\%$ at $25$k ($p{=}0.21$) and $+4.5\%$ fine-tuned ($p{=}0.19$): null, and
with its sign against pose on all three metrics. The rungs are not equally
sensitive, and we state what each one
could have seen: at $80\%$ power the minimum detectable content effect is
$3.5\%$ at $579$ scenes ($n{=}5$), $7.6\%$ at $5$k ($n{=}3$) and $2.2\%$ at
$25$k ($n{=}5$). The $5$k rung is therefore weak evidence on its own and we do
not count it as an independent null; the weight falls on the $25$k rung, which
is both the largest scale and the most sensitive. At $25$k the paired seed-level
interval on the content difference is $[-1.59\%,+1.70\%]$, so at the largest
scale we train the data exclude a content effect beyond roughly $1.7\%$ in either
direction, a bound tighter than the $1.6\%$ cost of the branch that would carry
it. The
pedestrian-resampling bootstrap gives a visibly narrower interval
($[-0.0040,+0.0047]$\,m, ${\approx}\pm1.2\%$); with five clusters it undercovers,
and we quote the wider seed-level bound throughout.

After pretraining a trajectory-only backbone on full WOMD and fine-tuning
($n{=}5$), the content test is $+0.88\%$ ($p{=}0.18$), again with its sign against
pose (Table~\ref{tab:core}).

Seed count decides what Table~\ref{tab:core} appears to say. On the first three
seeds the same runs read as \emph{pose harms} from scratch ($+1.85\%$) and
\emph{shuffled is best} after fine-tuning ($-1.28\%$); at $n{=}5$ both shrink
roughly fourfold. Both three-seed effects are significant under a
pedestrian-resampling bootstrap ($p{=}0.037$, $p{=}0.016$) and neither is under a
paired $t$ over the same seeds ($p{=}0.18$, $p{=}0.12$): three seeds do not
resolve a $1\%$ effect here, and a bootstrap over the wrong unit will not say so.

\subsection{Two effects that are real, and are not pose}
\label{sec:real}
\textbf{A branch-capacity tax.} At $25$k both pose arms are worse than no-pose
($+1.66\%$, $p{=}5{\times}10^{-4}$; $+1.63\%$, $p{=}0.048$) and indistinguishable
from each other. An extra input branch costs ${\sim}1.6\%$ whether it carries the
right body or the wrong one.

This is an operating-point statement, and the operating point matters: it is
measured at each run's best validation epoch. Models with wider inputs overfit
sooner, so the sign of a pose-versus-no-pose comparison is not stable under a
fixed late-epoch rule, where the no-pose arm has degraded further by then
($+8.0\%$ in favour of pose at epoch $30$, $n{=}3$; $+0.9\%$ at $n{=}8$, both
n.s. and neither resolved at these seed counts). What is stable is that the
comparison moves with the stopping rule, so we never compare architecturally
different arms at a fixed late epoch. The \emph{content} test is null under
either rule: its two arms have identical input dimensionality, so this asymmetry
does not arise. Replacing the $144$-d branch with eight frame-invariant descriptors
fused into the existing agent channels removes the cost ($-0.01\%$ versus
no-pose, $p{=}0.99$; $-1.67\%$ versus the branch, $p{=}0.12$ at $n{=}3$) without
creating a benefit: pose can be carried for free, and still does not pay. That
freedom is real, and Sec.~\ref{sec:earlyfloor} is what establishes it: fed an
uncorrupted oracle at $25$k scenes this pathway converts it on every seed, so a
channel it costs nothing to carry is still a channel it reads. We
quote the seed-level $p$ here for consistency with the rest of the paper; a
pedestrian-resampling bootstrap makes the branch contrast look decisive
($p{=}0.006$), which is exactly the unit error Sec.~\ref{sec:setup} warns
against.

\textbf{Tuning dominates the treatment.} At $5$k the learning rate inherited from
the smaller rung made pose look catastrophic ($+37\%$); with per-arm tuning,
$-0.90\%$, n.s. Across the $25$k grid the learning rate moves \minADE by up to
$16.7\%$ against a content signal of ${\sim}0.05\%$: a pose-versus-baseline
comparison that does not tune each arm separately is a claim about the learning
rate.

\subsection{The branch is not inert}
\label{sec:poscontrol}
A null measured through one pathway invites the obvious objection that the
pathway is dead. The shuffled control does not dispose of it: it shows the model
cannot distinguish its own body from a stranger's, which is equally consistent
with a branch that transmits nothing at all. The branch-capacity tax shows the
branch perturbs the output, not that it carries signal, and the fusion residual
is zero-initialised, so a dead branch and an uninformative one begin at the same
place.

We therefore ran the positive control. The \emph{oracle} arm replaces the SMPL
features with a six-channel encoding of the agent's own future displacement at
$3$ and $8$\,s, which is genuinely predictive by construction. It keeps the
branch, its parameter count, its input dimensionality and its per-frame validity
masks exactly as the pose arm has them, so the oracle is visible only where a
real pose would have been.

Over three seeds the oracle arm reaches $0.2945$ against no-pose's $0.4514$ on
the same seeds, a change of $-34.8\%$ ($t(2){=}-81.4$,
$p{=}1.5{\times}10^{-4}$), and $-35.6\%$ against the shuffled arm. The oracle is of course not a usable model,
since it reads the future; its only job is to calibrate the pathway. It does so
emphatically: the branch converts information into accuracy at roughly twenty
times the magnitude of the bound our content test places on pose. The null is
therefore a statement about what SMPL pose contains, not about whether the
architecture can use what it is given.

\subsection{Where a benefit could still hide}
\label{sec:hide}
On the confirmation runs the content test is null at every horizon and least
favourable at the shortest ($+0.48\%$ at $2$\,s, $-0.04\%$ at $8$\,s); null in
all $20$ tests across quartiles and top deciles of constant-velocity residual,
heading variation, speed range and peak acceleration; and null for
standing-then-moving pedestrians, the subpopulation built to favour it
($n{=}1{,}809$ pedestrians, $+0.38\%$, epoch-$30$ checkpoints on an expanded
validation split; $p{=}0.80$ by a pedestrian-resampling bootstrap, the one place
we quote one, because this subpopulation is defined per pedestrian).

It is also null where the body is best measured, which is where a
pose-quality explanation predicts an effect should appear. Stratifying the $25$k
content test by LiDAR return count gives $-0.37\%$ ($[-1.87,+1.14]$) on the
$2{,}372$ agents with at least $200$ returns and $-0.08\%$ on the $10{,}902$
with at least $30$; stratifying by range gives $-0.14\%$ ($[-1.61,+1.33]$)
inside $10$\,m. Nine strata, no effect, at $n{=}5$ throughout. Whatever the null
is, it is not only sparsity at range.

Across the roughly forty content tests this study reports, one reaches
$p{<}0.05$: in the stationary subpopulation of the heading ablation, correct
pose is $+1.8\%$ \emph{worse} than a stranger's ($n{=}8$, $p{=}0.011$). At that
multiplicity one such result is expected by chance, and its direction is against
a pose mechanism rather than for one; we report it so that the released metrics
contain no surprise.

\subsection{What the oracle ladder measures}
\label{sec:floor}
The content test bounds what pose is worth, and the oracle of
Sec.~\ref{sec:poscontrol} shows the branch can carry information. Neither says
what \emph{kind} of information this pathway turns into accuracy. We measured
that by degrading the oracle.

Each rung of Table~\ref{tab:floor} feeds the branch the same direction-only
oracle, the bearing of the agent's own future displacement at $3$ and $8$\,s,
corrupted by a fixed angular error. Each is labelled by the median
bearing error actually delivered: $0.6745\sigma$ by construction, since the
corruption is a fixed per-agent rotation, and measured to within $0.7^\circ$ of
it on $11{,}328$ scored agents. Displacement magnitude is withheld, so the
channel carries direction alone. The error is drawn once per pedestrian, not per
epoch: a real estimator's error is a property of the observation, and resampling
it would let training average it away.

At $25$k scenes every rung converts, and the gain stays large: $-34.50\%$ with an
exact bearing, $-19.77\%$ at $5.0^\circ$, $-15.68\%$ at $10.0^\circ$ and
$-13.57\%$ at $15.1^\circ$, all at $p\le0.0016$ against minimum detectable
effects of $1.4$--$2.9$\,pp. The decay is not exponential: one exponential fitted
to the four rungs leaves residuals (measured minus fitted) of $-1.5$, $+3.2$,
$+0.3$ and $-2.5$\,pp, a systematic pattern in $\theta$ against those MDEs rather
than scatter about the curve. The curve decelerates, and two terms fit
it to $0.4$\,pp:
\begin{equation}
\Delta\minADE(\theta) \;\approx\; -\big[\,13.1\% + 21.4\%\cdot e^{-\theta/4.4^\circ}\,\big],
\label{eq:floor}
\end{equation}
a precision term with a half-life of $3.0^\circ$ over a term the corruptions in
this ladder do not resolve. That term is slow, not permanent: it is flat to
$15.1^\circ$ here, while the same ladder at $579$ scenes reads $-0.1$ to
$+2.7\%$ from $24.9^\circ$ outward, with intervals that exclude the
${\approx}6\%$ a persistent term scaled from that ladder's own $\theta{=}0$
amplitude would predict (Table~\ref{tab:floor}). It must decay somewhere beyond
our rungs, since a $60^\circ$ median error is close to uniform on the circle. The seeds agree closely: $12.3$--$14.2\%$ persistent,
amplitudes $20.8$--$21.8\%$, half-lives $2.8$--$3.4^\circ$.

\textbf{The angular axis is not an acceptance threshold.} It is tempting to read
$\theta$ as the accuracy a cue must reach: feed the branch a channel whose
bearing error is $\theta$, expect the gain at rung $\theta$. The ladder refutes
that reading itself. The baseline already receives the tracker's box heading, as
a per-timestep sine--cosine pair for every agent (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}), and that
heading estimates the \emph{same} future displacement bearing at $3.9^\circ$ in
median, better than the $15.1^\circ$ rung's channel at every percentile
($9.3^\circ$ against $25.8^\circ$ at the third quartile, $20.1^\circ$ against
$36.8^\circ$ at the ninetieth). A channel uniformly less accurate than an input
the model already has still buys $-13.6\%$. Nor does the branch pay for merely
being occupied: an extra branch carrying uninformative content \emph{costs}
${\sim}1.6\%$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:real}), and the same branch fed another pedestrian's
pose returns $+0.04\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}). The $34.5\%$ is content, and the
only content this channel has is the realised future.

\textbf{The surviving term is future-side mode selection.} Eq.~(\ref{eq:floor})
splits the gain, and the two terms behave differently in ways that identify them.
Per-agent records at each run's own best epoch, paired between arms on
$16{,}486$ agents and averaged over seeds, put the persistent term at the end of
the trajectory: refitting the decomposition at each horizon leaves the precision
half-life at $2.9$--$3.9^\circ$ throughout, while the persistent share of the
gain rises from $21\%$ of the $2$\,s error reduction to $39\%$ at $8$\,s and
$53\%$ at the endpoint. The selection reading predicts exactly that, and it
predicts more: corrupting the bearing should barely affect \emph{which} of the
six modes is right. It does not. The gap between the top-ranked mode and the best
of six, which is what the forecaster loses by choosing wrongly, falls from $0.67$\,m to
$0.26$\,m with an exact oracle, and still to $0.29$\,m at $15.1^\circ$: the
corrupted channel closes $92\%$ of the perfect oracle's selection gap while
recovering $39\%$ of its \minADE. The absolute gain is also largest in the
quartile where the baseline's own modes disagree most at the endpoint
($0.150$\,m against $0.111$--$0.130$\,m elsewhere, at $\theta{=}0$). A coarse
future bearing says which way the agent went; only a precise one refines where
along that way, and that refinement is what $3^\circ$ of error destroys.

\minADE takes the best of six modes whatever their rank, so selection reaches it
only through the decoder's scoring step, which keeps six of $64$ intention
queries by predicted likelihood: a better ranking changes which six survive.
That is also where a pose effect could hide from our headline metric, so we
scored the ranking columns directly on the $25$k arms. They are null as well:
against a stranger's pose, top-$1$ ADE is $-2.07\%$ ($p{=}0.11$, MDE
$3.7$\,pp), best-mode rank $-0.93\%$ ($p{=}0.22$) and endpoint mode spread
$+0.17\%$ ($p{=}0.53$), over the same $16{,}486$ agents.

The ladder is therefore a positive control and an upper bound on what any
direction channel through this branch could buy \emph{if it knew the future},
and (\ref{eq:floor}) says how much of that bound is precision rather
than selection. An observation-time cue buys selection only to the extent that
it predicts the realised mode beyond what the model's existing inputs already
predict; the oracle's advantage is that it does not predict the future mode but
reads it.

\textbf{A null at low power looks like a threshold.} We first ran this ladder at
$579$ scenes, where it reads as a clean detection floor: significant at
$10.0^\circ$ ($-6.0\%$, $p{=}0.04$), indistinguishable from zero at $15.1^\circ$
($-1.4\%$, $p{=}0.47$), flat above. At $25$k that second rung is $-13.6\%$. The
$579$ ladder could never have seen it: scaling its own $\theta{=}0$ amplitude
($-15.8\%$) by the $25$k decay predicts ${\approx}6.2\%$ there, against that
rung's MDE of $6.7$\,pp. Its apparent floor was the angle at which a three-seed
experiment went blind, and a threshold read off the first non-significant rung is
a statement about power wearing the clothes of a property of the task. We report
both ladders in Table~\ref{tab:floor} for that reason.

\subsection{Why body facing does not pay}
\label{sec:belowfloor}
The oracle converts a bearing read off the realised future. Body facing is not
that: it is an observation-time estimate of a quantity the model already
measures. Three properties of it, on the population the ladder scores, say what
is left for it to contribute.

\emph{It is a noisier copy of an existing input.} The box heading estimates the
future displacement bearing at $3.9^\circ$ ($+3$\,s) and $7.1^\circ$ ($+8$\,s)
in median; SMPL facing reaches $12.3^\circ$ ($n{=}5{,}824$) and $15.1^\circ$
($n{=}6{,}206$). Box heading is the better estimate at every percentile we
measured, the two differ by $10.0^\circ$ in per-agent median, and both derive
from the same returns. We calibrated the SMPL forward axis \emph{against} the
box heading before measuring either.

\emph{Most of its accuracy is the trajectory restated.} $95.5\%$ of the scored
agents move, and for a mover the pipeline snaps root orientation to the direction
of travel whenever the raw estimate deviates by more than $90^\circ$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}). Movers sit at $11.9^\circ$; the $262$ stationary
agents, the only ones whose facing their velocity does not already imply, sit
at $41.2^\circ$. The pooled median largely measures the snapping, and the
distribution is heavy either way: $5.5^\circ$ at the first quartile, $23.9^\circ$
at the third, $45.9^\circ$ at the ninetieth percentile.

\emph{Where it would be non-redundant, it is worst.} On the one case where
velocity carries no directional information (which way a stationary pedestrian
steps off), SMPL facing predicts onset direction at roughly twice chance over
$19{,}939$ validation pedestrians: median error $44.2^\circ$, $50.6\%$ within
$45^\circ$ (chance $90^\circ$, $25\%$). Informative, consistent with the
$41.2^\circ$ above, and not an artifact of our axis fitting: the box heading
estimates the same quantity at $25.2^\circ$, and paired on the same events SMPL
is $7.3^\circ$ worse in median (Wilcoxon $p{=}2.6{\times}10^{-43}$). Nor does
pose take over where perception degrades: over $1{,}705$ onset events the gap
favours the box heading in all ten testable strata ($p<10^{-5}$ each) and
\emph{widens} with range, to $+32.9^\circ$ at $41$--$75$\,m, because both derive
from the same returns.

Facing is therefore redundant where it is accurate and inaccurate where it would
be non-redundant, and neither half of that squeeze is the selection signal the
oracle trades on. What survives it is what the content test measures directly: at
$25$k scenes $+0.04\%$, with the data bounding it to $\pm1.7\%$, inside the
${\sim}1.6\%$ cost of the branch that would carry it.

\textbf{Removing the redundant input does not rescue it.} Redundancy says why
little is left for facing to add; it is not by itself a mechanism that blocks
conversion. So we tested the implied counterfactual: delete the box-heading
channel, retrain all six cells, and repeat the content test, at the $579$-scene
rung and eight seeds. Nothing appears, anywhere. The difference in content effect
between the two conditions is $+2.9$\,pp on the $13{,}006$ moving
agents, \emph{the wrong sign} for a benefit that redundancy had been masking,
and $-0.6$\,pp on the stationary ones ($p{=}0.65$). Seed-level
intervals at this scale are wide (${\pm}2$ to ${\pm}18$\,pp, widest exactly where
\minADE is largest), so we also average the eight runs per agent and test over
agents, which cuts run noise without licensing a claim about retraining: that
gives $[+2.4,+3.3]$\,pp on movers, $[-1.5,+0.2]$ on the stationary subset, and
$-1.2$\,pp with $[-2.6,+0.2]$ in the top decile of turning, the stratum where
neither velocity nor heading states the future bearing. The two analyses agree to
within $0.15$\,pp on every stratum.

We can also say how much this ablation could ever have shown: deleting the heading
channel costs the no-pose baseline only $1.5\%$ on movers, because velocity stays
in the input, so there was never much room for facing to fill. What remains
unexcluded is the reading the content test's own bound asserts: that pose carries
nothing this forecaster can use at any level of redundancy. At $n{=}3$ this
comparison read $-4.1$\,pp with all three seeds agreeing in sign: the third
multi-percent effect here to dissolve under more seeds.

\subsection{The cheaper pathway converts a bearing, and still not pose}
\label{sec:earlyfloor}
Early fusion removes the branch tax (Sec.~\ref{sec:real}), which makes it the
pathway worth recommending, so we ran the same ladder through it, carrying the
oracle in the per-timestep agent channels instead of the branch. At a given rung
the six channels equal the branch's six inputs bit for bit, so the rungs keep
their measured-error labels.

At $579$ scenes this ladder reads as a dead pathway. No rung is significant,
\emph{including the uncorrupted one}: $-4.5\%$ at $\theta{=}0$ ($p{=}0.44$),
$-1.0\%$ at $5.0^\circ$ ($p{=}0.53$) and $+0.2\%$ at $10.0^\circ$ ($p{=}0.94$),
and the per-seed pattern looks like a mechanism rather than noise. The side
branch converts the uncorrupted channel on all three seeds ($-16.5\%$,
$-19.3\%$, $-11.5\%$) while early fusion converts it on one ($-14.0\%$, against
$+0.7\%$ and $-0.3\%$), with the two failing seeds sitting at their no-pose level
rather than scattering about it. A difference in \emph{how many seeds move at
all} does not look like something a wide interval explains away.

It was. The MDE at $\theta{=}0$ is $25.5$\,pp, and we re-ran the rung at $25$k
scenes, where the branch ladder's MDE was $1.4$--$2.9$\,pp. Early fusion converts
the uncorrupted channel there on \emph{every} seed, by $-31.5\%$
($p{=}0.011$, $95\%$ CI $[-38.2,-24.8]$), against the branch's $-34.5\%$ at the
same rung and denominator. So the $579$ reading was the same power artifact
twice over: once in the significance column, and once in a per-seed signature we
trusted because it was not a $p$-value. Three runs at $579$ scenes cannot tell a
pathway that ignores a channel from one that converts it by a third.

This also settles the free-carriage result of Sec.~\ref{sec:real} the other way.
``No tax, no benefit'' admitted two readings: that early fusion integrates pose
at no capacity cost, or that it largely ignores the appended channels, so that
there is nothing to cost and nothing to gain. A perfect channel separates them,
and at the scale where we can resolve the difference the first reading is
correct. Early fusion reads what it is given. Pose through that pathway is
therefore a cue that arrives, is used when it carries something, and still buys
nothing, which removes the last place the null could have been an artifact of
integration rather than a property of the cue.

\subsection{The complementary cue is barely measured}
Gaze precedes locomotion, and head orientation is an established intent cue for
vehicle-based pedestrian path
prediction~\cite{kooij2014context,flohr2015orientation}, so head yaw relative to
the torso is a plausible \emph{independent} cue. It is very nearly not
recoverable here. In the
fitted meshes head-versus-torso yaw has a standard deviation of $3.8^\circ$,
while against the ground-truth 3D keypoints released with Waymo
Perception~\cite{sun2020waymoperception,zheng2022keypoints} ($n{=}8{,}234$) the
real quantity varies $19.2^\circ$: a fivefold under-dispersion, so the estimator
is largely emitting its prior. Consistently, head yaw carries no usable signal
for onset direction ($r{=}-0.03$, n.s.), and the head is the one joint in
Table~\ref{tab:skill} whose interval includes zero. At $20$--$40$\,m the head is
a near-spherical handful of points with little geometry to constrain yaw.

\subsection{MPJPE is the wrong acceptance metric}
A single mean error cannot distinguish an estimator that tracks a joint
imprecisely from one that emits its prior. We therefore score each joint against
the strongest trivial competitor, always predicting the mean root-aligned pose:
$\mathrm{SKILL}_j = 1 - \mathrm{MPJPE}_j / \mathrm{MP}_j$, where values at or
below zero mean the estimator contributes nothing at that joint.

Evaluated on Waymo Perception against ground-truth keypoints ($1{,}789$ frames,
$534$ pedestrians), every limb joint scores $+0.457$ to $+0.639$ (Table~\ref{tab:skill}). The head
scores $+0.045$, yet its MPJPE ($111.6$\,mm) is \emph{better} than either wrist
($118.3$/$125.4$\,mm). Its error is flattering not because it is well estimated
but because it barely improves on its own mean-pose baseline ($116.9$\,mm),
whereas the hips, whose ground-truth spread is \emph{smaller} at $97.9$\,mm, are
predicted at $39.8$\,mm. Across the thirteen joints MPJPE and skill are
essentially uncorrelated (Spearman $\rho{=}-0.11$, $p{=}0.72$): the aggregate is
not merely a noisy proxy for information content but carries almost none of it,
and for the head it actively inverts the ordering. We recommend reporting
per-joint skill alongside it whenever a pose estimator is accepted into a
downstream stack.

\subsection{A general condition}
Two results locate where pose could have helped. At $2$\,s, \minADE is
$0.091$\,m, and a perfect future-direction oracle through the same branch removes
only $1.4$\,cm of it ($-14.7\%$): relative headroom exists at short horizons, but
in absolute terms there is almost nothing for any cue to win. This is a property of the forecaster's own residual, not
of constant velocity: single-mode constant-velocity
extrapolation~\cite{scholler2020constant} gives $0.310$\,m at $2$\,s on the same
set, so short-horizon forecasting is \emph{not} CV-saturated (a comparison that
favours the model, since \minADE takes the best of $K{=}6$ modes). Saturation
here means the baseline's own remaining error is a few percent of the
displacement. At long horizons the residual is dominated by destination and
intent, which body configuration does not disclose; stratifying by how badly
constant velocity fails still yields no effect.

In general form, and offered as a hypothesis from one modality on one dataset
rather than a law: \emph{an auxiliary modality improves a forecast only where the
baseline's own residual leaves room in absolute terms, where that residual is not
dominated by intent the modality cannot observe, and where the modality carries
information about the cue that the model's existing inputs do not already carry.}
All three clauses must hold. The third is not a comparison of marginal
accuracies, and this paper is the reason to state it that way:
Sec.~\ref{sec:floor} converts a channel less accurate at every percentile than an
input the model already has, so being the noisier estimate is not by itself
disqualifying. What disqualifies facing is that its error is \emph{shared} with
the input it would supplement. It is calibrated against the box heading, snapped
to the direction of travel for the $95.5\%$ of agents that move, and recovered
from the same returns, so where it is accurate it is redundant and where it would
be independent it is worst (Sec.~\ref{sec:belowfloor}). The check this licenses
is still cheap and still skipped, and it is still a measurement on the dataset
with no training: how much of the target does the candidate cue explain that the
existing inputs do not?

\section{What This Means in Practice}
For an AV stack, four consequences follow. First, before integrating an
auxiliary modality, measure not only its error on the cue it is meant to carry
but how much of that cue it explains \emph{beyond the inputs the model already
receives}. That costs no training, and for pose it is the measurement that would
have predicted the outcome. Second, an oracle ladder is still worth its handful
of runs, as a positive control and as an upper bound on the pathway, but size it
so its MDE is below the effect you would act on, and do not read its angular axis
as an acceptance threshold: ours converts a channel less accurate than an input
the model already has, because an oracle knows which future occurred and no
sensor does (Sec.~\ref{sec:floor}). Third, the
capacity cost of an extra branch is real (${\sim}1.6\%$ here, at the best-epoch
operating point) and is easily mistaken for a modality effect in either
direction; early fusion avoids it while still reading the channel, which an
oracle through that pathway is what demonstrates, and a shuffled-pose control
separates a benefit from a capacity effect either way. Fourth, do not accept a pose estimator on aggregate MPJPE alone: check
per-joint skill, because the joints an aggregate flatters may be the ones
carrying a prior.

\textbf{Limitations.} The largest rung we train is $25$k scenes of the
$299{,}220$ exported, so the full rung is a compute limit rather than an
annotation one; at eight seeds the minimum detectable content effect is
${\approx}1.25\%$ at $80\%$ power, and the ladders run at $n{=}3$ per rung
(Table~\ref{tab:floor}). We test one forecaster, one dataset and one pose
estimator. We ladder two integration pathways rather than one, but two is not
every way a cue can enter a model, and only the late-fusion ladder is resolved at
$25$k at every rung; the early-fusion ladder is resolved at $25$k only at
$\theta{=}0$, and its corrupted rungs remain at $579$ scenes, where this paper's
own power argument applies to them (Sec.~\ref{sec:earlyfloor}). The redundancy
counterfactual, which deletes the heading channel to see whether facing then
pays, is run at the $579$-scene rung rather than at $25$k, and its seed-level
intervals there are wide enough that the agent-level analysis carries the turn
strata. WOMD ships camera data only as
quantised tokens, so a camera-based pose or gaze estimate cannot be tested at
this scale; on Waymo Perception, where raw imagery exists, an RGB estimator
(HMR2.0) reaches $106.2$\,mm root-aligned MPJPE against LiDAR-HMR's $87.6$\,mm
on the same $347$ frames, so the obvious better-pose route is the worse one
here. We do not model pose--pose interaction.

\section{Conclusion}
Body pose, recovered from LiDAR, does not improve pedestrian trajectory
forecasting on WOMD. The null is controlled against a shuffled-pose baseline and
holds across scale, representation and integration; an oracle through the same
branch rules out a dead
pathway. It also has a quantitative mechanism. An oracle ladder shows this integration
path converts a direction channel at every accuracy we trained, including one
uniformly worse than the tracker heading the model already receives, so the
pathway is not the obstacle and no attainable pose accuracy is the remedy. What
the oracle sells is knowledge of which future occurred: half its endpoint gain is
mode selection that no corruption in our ladder resolves, against a fifth at
$2$\,s, and a channel corrupted to $15.1^\circ$ still closes $92\%$ of the
selection gap. Body facing cannot supply that at any accuracy, and as a bearing
it is a noisier copy of one the model already has, and worst, at $41.2^\circ$, on
the stationary pedestrians the cue was introduced to serve. Fusing the same cue early removes the branch's capacity cost and does
not rescue it either, and the reason is not that the cheaper pathway ignores its
input: fed an uncorrupted bearing it converts it on every seed, by a third. Both
integration routes read what they are given, and neither finds anything in the
body to read. The lesson generalises past pose. Before integrating a modality, measure
how much of the cue it is meant to carry it explains beyond the model's existing
inputs, and make sure any ladder you judge it against can resolve the effect you
would act on.
