\begin{abstract}
Gait and facing appear to encode pedestrian intent that a position history
lacks, which makes body pose an attractive forecasting input. On the Waymo Open
Motion Dataset (WOMD), with SMPL poses recovered from LiDAR, we find that
whether pose pays depends on the track it is added to and on what the pose
pipeline was itself given. The decisive comparison is correct pose against the
same architecture fed another pedestrian's pose. On WOMD's tracks, offboard
auto-labels refined with future frames, it returns $+0.04\%$ ($p{=}0.93$) at
our largest scale, bounded to $\pm1.7\%$. With the history a causal onboard
tracker would report, own pose beats a stranger's by $5.3\%$ ($p{=}0.002$) at
$30$\,cm of detection noise. Three quarters of that gain is the label returning
through the body: the pipeline's motion-prior fit matches the gait to the
offboard track. With poses that never saw the track the gain is $1.3\%$
($p{=}0.10$), and $3.5\%$ ($p{=}0.010$, uncorrected) on pedestrians with at
least $200$ LiDAR returns. On $13{,}937$ such pedestrians in held-out scenes
it is $2.1\%$ ($p{=}0.12$) and rises with the tracker's velocity error, from
$0.0\%$ in the lowest quartile to $4.9\%$ in the highest ($p{<}0.001$). Own
pose does not beat no pose there ($+0.9\%$, $p{=}0.67$). An oracle through
the same branch converts the agent's own future bearing, so the pathway is not
the obstacle. We recommend measuring what a modality carries beyond the
model's existing inputs at the quality a vehicle will have, and checking which
labels its own pipeline consumed.
\end{abstract}

pedestrian trajectory prediction, human pose estimation, LiDAR, autonomous driving, offboard labels

\section{Introduction}
Predicting where a pedestrian will go is a safety-critical function of an
automated vehicle, and body pose is an obvious candidate input. A person's stance,
gait phase and facing direction plausibly reveal intent before their position
changes~\cite{kooij2014context,flohr2015orientation}, and modern perception stacks already produce human mesh or keypoint
estimates. Several recent systems condition trajectory forecasts on
pose~\cite{saadatnejad2024socialtransmotion,gao2025socialpose,salzmann2023robots,kress2022pose}.

This paper reports that, on the labelled tracks of the Waymo Open Motion
Dataset (WOMD), pose conditioning does not measurably improve pedestrian
trajectory accuracy, and that the reason is the track it is added to. WOMD's boxes are offboard
auto-labels, refined with the whole track, future frames
included~\cite{ettinger2021waymo,qi2021offboard}. Against that track the body
restates what the model already knows. Against the history a causal onboard
tracker would report, the same body appears to pay, and most of that gain is the
offboard label returning through the pose pipeline, which fits the gait to the
track. Poses that never saw the track beat a stranger's where the body is
resolved and the tracker is wrong, and do not beat no pose on held-out scenes
(Fig.~\ref{fig:gabs}).

Our contributions are threefold. First, a controlled null. Against the same
model fed another pedestrian's body, pose adds nothing on WOMD's offboard tracks
at three training scales, while an oracle through the same branch improves
\minADE by $34.8\%$. Second, an onboard-quality track and a label leak. Under a
simulated causal tracker own pose beats a stranger's by $5.3\%$, but three
quarters of that is the track label returning through the pose pipeline, and
without it own pose beats a stranger's only where the tracker is wrong, and
does not beat no pose on held-out scenes. Third, a degraded
oracle shows that the branch converts even a coarse future direction, so the
null reflects what body facing carries beyond the box heading.

\section{Related Work}
Trajectory forecasting on WOMD is dominated by anchor- and query-based
transformers. We build on MTR~\cite{shi2022mtr}, which is
representative and strong. Adding raw LiDAR features to a WOMD forecaster
leaves pedestrian \minADE at $0.54$\,m~\cite{chen2024womdlidar}. Human mesh recovery from LiDAR is
maturing~\cite{fan2023lidarhmr}, with motion priors completing sparse
observations~\cite{he2022nemf,mahmood2019amass}. Intent-focused
work on pedestrian datasets~\cite{rasouli2019pie,fang2018pedestrian} targets
crossing decisions rather than metric trajectories. Kress
et al.~\cite{kress2022pose} report pose-based gains for vulnerable road users
from a research vehicle, at close range and with camera-derived pose.
Methodologically our closest precedent is Makansi
et al.~\cite{makansi2022walkalone}, who find by feature attribution that
trajectory predictors draw almost nothing from an input the field had assumed
was central.

\textbf{Relation to prior positive results.} Pose conditioning has reported real
gains, and our mechanism predicts where they come from rather than contradicting
them. Social-Transmotion~\cite{saadatnejad2024socialtransmotion} lowers ADE on
the synthetic JTA dataset from $0.99$ to $0.89$ with ground-truth 3D pose
and from $0.40$ to $0.36$ on JRDB with estimated pose.
Social-Pose~\cite{gao2025socialpose} reports about $25\%$ on JTA
($1.20{\to}0.90$) and on JRDB. Salzmann et al.~\cite{salzmann2023robots} report
${\approx}10\%$ from onboard-estimated keypoints at ranges up to $7$\,m, for
agents with little position history. A short history is a poor track and $7$\,m
is a well-resolved body, the one condition under which our track-free poses
appear to pay (Sec.~\ref{sec:onboard}). Le et al.~\cite{le2026legsoverarms}
localise the JRDB gain to lower-body 3D keypoints ($13\%$ ADE).

The two vehicle-captured settings closest to ours report small effects.
Li et al.~\cite{li2023keypoints} add 3D keypoints to a predictor on the Waymo
perception set, and $4$\,s \minADE moves from $0.249$ to $0.239$\,m in a
single run.
Social-Pose also evaluates on Pedestrians and Cyclists in Road Traffic, and
with ground-truth 3D pose ADE moves
from $0.58$ to $0.57$\,m, by $0.01$\,m or less for all five architectures
tested, against the ${\approx}25\%$ the same encoder delivers on JTA. Two
properties separate the remaining positive settings from an AV stack.
The first is range and sensor. JRDB is an indoor and campus robot dataset in which
pedestrians are metres away and pose is camera-derived, whereas WOMD
pedestrians are observed from $2$ to $75$\,m and often return a few dozen LiDAR
points. The second is the baseline. Those predictors consume positions, not a box
heading, so body facing is new information there and partly duplicated here.
None reports a shuffled-pose arm.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Data}
We use WOMD~\cite{ettinger2021waymo} with SMPL~\cite{loper2015smpl} poses
recovered from LiDAR by the Waymo-3DSkelMo pipeline~\cite{zhu2025waymo3dskelmo},
which fits per-frame bodies with LiDAR-HMR~\cite{fan2023lidarhmr} and completes
them with a NeMF motion prior trained on
AMASS~\cite{he2022nemf,mahmood2019amass}. We run its released second
version, whose fit adds a term that keeps the joints inside the labelled box.
Poses cover the $11$
observed frames, and the prediction horizon is $8$\,s at $10$\,Hz. In every
arm the model sees the map and the pedestrians that carry poses. Vehicles,
cyclists and the ego vehicle are not in its input. Every predicted agent
carries at least one valid pose frame, and $86.7\%$ of observed frames carry
one.

One property of that pipeline bears on Sec.~\ref{sec:why}: when a pedestrian is
moving and the raw LiDAR estimate of root rotation deviates from the
past-trajectory direction by more than $90^\circ$, the pipeline snaps the root to
the direction of travel. Body facing is therefore partly trajectory-derived for
moving pedestrians. This reinforces the redundancy we report rather than
weakening it, and it does not reach the stationary-onset analysis, whose events
are all below $0.3$\,m/s. A second property bears on Sec.~\ref{sec:onboard}: the
motion-prior stage fits each sequence against the box track, so the completed
body is not independent of that track. We call the per-frame LiDAR-HMR output
that precedes it the pre-fit pose (Fig.~\ref{fig:pcmesh}).

Applying the pipeline to WOMD requires one correction. Its export groups pose
clips by object identifier, and WOMD identifiers are unique only within a scene,
so nearly every training sequence carries another pedestrian's body. Grouping per scene ($299{,}220$ scenes, $1.81$M
sequences) cuts the median error between root heading and travel direction from
$41.5^\circ$ to $11.4^\circ$ and raises the gait--speed correlation from
$r{=}0.26$ to $0.58$. The uncorrected export fails silently, since training
converges and losses look normal. All results use the corrected export, in
which the body's root is nearer to its own track's box centre than to any other
agent's on $99.0\%$ of posed validation frames.

\subsection{Arms and the content test}
Three arms share one architecture (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). The no-pose arm receives trajectories and map.
The pose arm adds the pedestrian's own SMPL sequence, $144$ joint-rotation
values per frame with no translation, through a cross-attention encoder with
sinusoidal positional encoding. The shuffled arm receives
another pedestrian's sequence, assigned once over the whole split by a fixed
derangement, with identical parameter count, optimiser and
input dimensionality. The root orientation enters in world coordinates while
the trajectories are agent-centric, so this arm does not state facing relative
to the model's frame. The agent-frame-root encoding does, and its content test
is null too ($+0.68\%$, $p{=}0.49$, $n{=}3$, MDE $4.3$\,pp). A fourth arm, the oracle, replaces the SMPL features with a six-channel
encoding of the agent's own future displacement at $3$ and $8$\,s, leaving the
branch and its masks untouched. It shows the pathway can carry information at
all (Sec.~\ref{sec:poscontrol}). Every arm but the no-pose arm, with pose by branch or by early fusion, also
predicts a future pose sequence under an auxiliary loss (a geodesic rotation
term and a mixture likelihood term, weight $0.1$ each): the stranger's in the
shuffled arm and the pedestrian's own elsewhere, the oracle included.

Pose versus no-pose confounds information with branch capacity and the auxiliary
loss, while pose versus shuffled holds both fixed and isolates content. We call the latter the content test and treat it
as decisive. Relative differences are $(A-B)/B$, so negative favours $A$.

Inference is over seeds, the unit the treatment is applied to. Unless flagged,
every comparison between trained arms is a paired $t$-test on per-seed
differences.

\subsection{Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
Every arm trains for $30$ epochs with AdamW (two scenes per GPU with at most $24$ predicted pedestrians each, weight decay
$0.01$, gradient-norm clipping $1.0$) under a step schedule that halves the
learning rate at epochs $10$, $20$ and $25$, and the decoder predicts $K{=}6$ modes.
The learning rate is chosen per scale from a grid run separately for each arm:
$1{\times}10^{-4}$ at $579$ scenes, $5{\times}10^{-5}$ at $5$k and
$2.5{\times}10^{-5}$ at $25$k, where every arm peaks at the same interior
optimum. Validation is a fixed $5{,}171$ of the $27{,}156$ exported validation scenes. Of their $17{,}218$ predicted pedestrians, the $16{,}486$ with at least one valid future step are scored. Headline
numbers are taken at each run's best validation epoch
over a fixed evaluation schedule, a selection made on the reported set and
identically for every arm, and Sec.~\ref{sec:real} reports what changes
under a fixed-epoch rule. Official WOMD mAP needs the full retained prediction
set, which our per-epoch evaluation does not store, so we report \minADE,
\minFDE and miss rate at $2$\,m. \minADE averages over a
pedestrian's valid future steps and \minFDE is taken at the last valid one, and
$36\%$ of the scored pedestrians have a full $8$\,s future. Code, configs, per-seed metrics
and the analysis scripts are released with the paper.

\subsection{The null on offboard tracks, and its bound}
Table~\ref{tab:ladder} reports all three arms at three scales. Data alone improves
the baseline by $19.2\%$, while the content test is null at every scale, in
\minADE, in \minFDE ($+0.45\%$, $-0.28\%$ and $+0.33\%$, $p\ge0.61$) and in miss
rate at $2$\,m, where it is
$+2.2\%$ at $25$k ($p{=}0.21$) and $+4.3\%$ fine-tuned ($p{=}0.19$): null, and
with its sign against pose on all three metrics. At $80\%$ power the minimum
detectable content effect is
$3.5\%$ at $579$ scenes ($n{=}5$), $7.6\%$ at $5$k ($n{=}3$) and $2.2\%$ at
$25$k ($n{=}5$). The $5$k scale is weak evidence on its own, and the weight
falls on $25$k, the largest scale and the most sensitive. There the paired
seed-level interval on the content difference is $[-1.59\%,+1.70\%]$. The
validation scenes contribute less: resampling them with the seeds fixed gives
$[-0.4\%,+0.5\%]$. The
data thus exclude a content effect beyond roughly $1.7\%$ in either direction, and
the largest benefit the interval allows is no larger than the $1.6\%$ cost of
the branch that would carry it.

After pretraining a trajectory-only backbone on full WOMD and fine-tuning at
$579$ scenes ($n{=}5$), the content test is $+0.88\%$ ($p{=}0.18$), again with
its sign against pose.

\subsection{Two effects that are real, and are not pose}
\label{sec:real}
\textbf{A branch-capacity cost.} At $25$k both pose arms are worse than no-pose
($+1.66\%$ at $p{=}5{\times}10^{-4}$ and $+1.63\%$ at $p{=}0.048$) and indistinguishable
from each other. An extra input branch costs ${\sim}1.6\%$ whether it carries the
right body or the wrong one. Both arms also train with the auxiliary pose loss,
which we did not separate from the branch at this scale.

This is measured at each run's best validation epoch. Under a fixed
late-epoch rule the sign of a pose-versus-no-pose comparison is not stable (at
epoch $30$ pose is $8.0\%$ better with $n{=}3$ and $0.9\%$ worse with $n{=}8$,
both n.s.), so outside the held-out pass of Sec.~\ref{sec:onboard} we never
compare architecturally different arms at a fixed late epoch. The content test is not significant under
either rule, though it is noisier at epoch $30$ ($-5.9\%$, $-4.7\%$ and
$-1.7\%$ at the three scales, $p{=}0.16$, $0.07$ and $0.60$). Replacing the $144$-d branch with eight frame-invariant descriptors
fused into the existing agent channels removes the cost ($-0.01\%$ versus
no-pose at $p{=}0.99$, and $-1.67\%$ versus the branch at $p{=}0.12$, $n{=}3$) without
creating a benefit: pose can be carried for free, and still does not pay. Fed
an uncorrupted oracle at $25$k scenes this pathway converts it on every seed,
by $-31.2\%$ ($p{=}0.0002$) against the branch's $-34.5\%$, so a channel it
costs nothing to carry is still a channel it reads.

\textbf{Tuning dominates the treatment.} Across the $25$k grid the learning rate
moves \minADE by up to $16.7\%$ against a content signal of ${\sim}0.05\%$, so
every arm is tuned separately.

\subsection{The branch is not inert}
\label{sec:poscontrol}
A null measured through one pathway invites the objection that the pathway is
dead, and the shuffled control does not dispose of it: a branch that transmits
nothing would also fail to tell its own body from a stranger's.

The oracle arm of Sec.~\ref{sec:setup} is the positive control. It is
predictive by construction, and it keeps the branch, its
parameter count, its input dimensionality and its per-frame validity masks
exactly as the pose arm has them, so the oracle is visible only where a real
pose would have been.

Over three seeds at $579$ scenes the oracle arm changes \minADE by $-34.8\%$
against no pose ($t(2){=}-81.4$, $p{=}1.5{\times}10^{-4}$) and by $-35.6\%$
against the shuffled arm. The oracle calibrates the pathway: the
branch converts information into accuracy at roughly twenty
times the magnitude of the bound our content test places on pose. The null is
therefore a statement about what SMPL pose contains, not about whether the
architecture can use what it is given.

\subsection{Where a benefit could still hide}
\label{sec:hide}
On an eight-seed replication of the content test at $30$ epochs, scored on a
held-out half of validation (about $8{,}040$ agents), it is null at every horizon and
least favourable at the shortest ($+0.48\%$ at $2$\,s, $-0.04\%$ at $8$\,s).
It is null in all $20$ tests across quartiles and top deciles of
constant-velocity residual, heading variation, speed range and peak
acceleration, on the $6{,}764$ of those agents for which the four are defined. It is also null for standing-then-moving pedestrians, the
subpopulation built to favour it ($n{=}1{,}809$ pedestrians, $+0.38\%$,
epoch-$30$ checkpoints on all $27{,}156$ validation scenes, $p{=}0.80$ by a
pedestrian-resampling bootstrap).

It is also null where the body is best measured, which is where a
pose-quality explanation predicts an effect should appear. Stratifying the $25$k
content test by LiDAR return count gives $-0.37\%$ ($[-1.87,+1.14]$) on the
$2{,}372$ agents with at least $200$ returns and $-0.08\%$ on the $10{,}902$
with at least $30$, and stratifying by range gives $-0.14\%$ ($[-1.61,+1.33]$)
inside $10$\,m. No stratum shows an effect, at $n{=}5$ throughout.

Across the more than forty content tests this study reports outside the
onboard-track ladder of Sec.~\ref{sec:onboard}, two reach $p{<}0.05$, and both
favour the stranger. In the stationary subpopulation of the
heading ablation, correct pose is $+1.8\%$ worse than a stranger's
($n{=}8$, $p{=}0.011$), and in the stationary subset of the frame-rotation
control at $\sigma{=}30^\circ$ it is $+1.3\%$ worse ($n{=}3$, $p{=}0.049$,
Sec.~\ref{sec:belowfloor}). At that multiplicity one or two such results are
expected by chance, and their direction is against a pose mechanism rather than
for one. The tests of Sec.~\ref{sec:onboard} that favour pose are a different
kind of result: they grow with the noise and agree in sign on every seed, and
most of what they measure is a label, not a body.

\subsection{An onboard-quality track, and a label leak}
\label{sec:onboard}
Everything above is measured on WOMD's tracks, and those are offboard
auto-labels. An offline network refines every box with the whole track, future
frames included~\cite{ettinger2021waymo,qi2021offboard}, and the velocity the
baseline receives is exactly the backward difference of those refined positions
at all $954{,}751$ pedestrian time steps we checked. The observed history is as
clean as the labeller could make it. A deployed stack has a causal tracker
instead, so we simulate one. Each observed position receives detection noise
$\mathcal{N}(0,s^2)$ per axis, drawn once rather than per epoch, and a
forward-only constant-velocity Kalman filter, started at rest at the first
observed frame (acceleration
noise $1$\,m/s$^2$, initial velocity uncertainty $1.5$\,m/s), turns the
detections into the positions and velocities the model sees. The box heading and
the future targets are untouched. At prediction time, measured on $675$
pedestrians, the tracker is off
by $7.5$\,cm and $0.23$\,m/s in median at $s{=}10$\,cm, and by $21$\,cm and
$0.46$\,m/s at $30$\,cm, against a median pedestrian speed of $1.1$\,m/s.

With the pipeline's fitted poses the null breaks (Table~\ref{tab:onboard}). At
$30$\,cm own pose beats a stranger's by $5.32\%$ ($p{=}0.002$, $95\%$ CI
$[-7.3,-3.4]$) and beats no pose by $4.06\%$ ($p{=}0.004$), over five seeds.
The mean holds at every fixed late epoch
($-5.2$ to $-5.8\%$ at epochs $26$, $28$ and $30$). It
sits where gait would put it: on the $8{,}757$ pedestrians walking at $0.8$ to
$1.6$\,m/s it is $-6.8\%$ ($p{=}0.0002$), while on the $3{,}336$ standing at
prediction time it is not significant.

\textbf{Most of it is the label.} The fitted poses are not independent of the
track. The motion-prior stage~\cite{he2022nemf} is a latent optimisation whose
loss predicts the root translation from the local body motion and matches it to
the box track, so the fit pulls the gait toward a stride that reproduces the
offboard displacement. The fit runs on the observed second alone, so no future frame enters, and
the model input holds joint rotations only, so auditing the input does not
reveal it.
Derived inputs have carried labels before: central-difference velocities gave
Trajectron++ the next position~\cite{trajectron2021derivatives}. A check
without training does. On the $11{,}588$ validation pedestrians with a pose on every observed frame, gradient-boosted
trees predict the clean one-second speed from body pose alone, root excluded,
with $R^2{=}0.94$ from the fitted pose and $0.44$ from the pre-fit one. On
moving pedestrians with fewer than $30$ LiDAR returns it is $0.78$ against less
than zero: the fitted body knows a speed the sensor could not have shown it.
Added to a regressor on the $30$\,cm tracker's own state, the fitted pose
removes $71\%$ of the squared speed error that regressor leaves, the pre-fit
pose about a fifth and a stranger's $1\%$.

We therefore train both pose arms at $30$\,cm on the pre-fit poses, which see
only the points inside the box. Files, agents, tracks and the stranger assigned
to each pedestrian are unchanged, and the two shuffled arms agree ($+0.40\%$,
$p{=}0.54$). Own pose then beats a stranger's by $1.34\%$ ($p{=}0.10$, CI
$[-3.1,+0.4]$) and does not beat no pose ($+0.36\%$, $p{=}0.54$). At fixed epochs $26$,
$28$ and $30$ it reads $-1.4$, $-2.6$ and $-1.5\%$, significant at the second
only ($p{=}0.02$). The fit
accounts for $3.98$ of the $5.32$ points ($p{=}0.0001$, between $3.5$ and $4.9$
on every seed), and on walkers $-6.8\%$ becomes $-0.8\%$ ($p{=}0.36$).

\textbf{What survives appears to follow the sensor.} Split by mean LiDAR returns over the
observed second, the pre-fit content effect is $-0.3\%$ below $30$ returns,
$-1.0\%$ and $-1.4\%$ in the two middle strata, and $-3.47\%$ ($p{=}0.010$, CI
$[-5.6,-1.4]$) on the $2{,}365$ pedestrians with at least $200$ that every run scores, where it also
beats no pose ($-1.99\%$, $p{=}0.041$). The top stratum is better than the
bottom on every seed, by $3.1$ points ($p{=}0.004$), and at every fixed late
epoch it reads $-3.2$ to $-4.6\%$. At those epochs the bottom stratum reads
$-1.4$ to $-2.1\%$ (n.s.), so the gap narrows to $1.7$ to $2.5$ points. The fitted poses show no gradient ($-5.3$,
$-5.4$, $-4.1$ and $-6.0\%$, $p{=}0.41$), as a leak that does not depend on the
sensor would not. On the offboard track the same stratum is null
(Sec.~\ref{sec:hide}).

\textbf{It follows the tracker's error, not the manoeuvre.}
Table~\ref{tab:dyn} splits the $2{,}272$ well-resolved pedestrians with at least
$1$\,s of future two ways and scores own pose against no pose, $-2.1\%$ on all
of them ($p{=}0.039$). By how wrong the tracker is at prediction time, the gain
is absent in the quartile with the smallest velocity error ($+0.4\%$) and
$-3.3\%$ in the largest, a difference of $3.7$ points ($p{=}0.013$), and
$+0.1\%$ against $-5.0\%$ over position error ($5.1$ points, $p{=}0.019$,
examples in Fig.~\ref{fig:onboardex}). The trend holds
on complete one-second histories alone ($4.0$ points, $p{=}0.019$). By what
the pedestrian goes on to do, the four manoeuvre measures of
Sec.~\ref{sec:hide}, the gain is largest in the lowest quartile of every
measure ($-3.4$ to $-5.6\%$) and shrinks toward the highest, by $3.8$ points
over constant-velocity residual ($p{=}0.048$) and $4.1$ over speed range
($p{=}0.019$). Against a stranger's pose, which holds the branch fixed, own
pose is ahead in all $24$ quartiles, with the same trend over tracker error
($3.1$ and $5.5$ points, both $p{=}0.001$) and none over the manoeuvre
($p\ge0.19$).

Three caveats bound this. The tracker is simulated, with Gaussian detection
noise rather than a detector's correlated, occlusion-driven errors, and it
never switches or fragments an identity, errors that degrade forecasts on real
tracker output~\cite{weng2022mtp}. It also never follows a pedestrian for more
than a second. On a steadily walking pedestrian the same filter's median
velocity error at $30$\,cm is $0.33$\,m/s after one second and $0.16$\,m/s
after three, so a longer-lived track leaves pose less to repair. The pre-fit
poses are per-frame and unsmoothed, so a prior fitted to the body alone might
recover more. The fit used here does not: on $1{,}789$ keypoint-labelled frames
of the perception validation
set~\cite{sun2020waymoperception,zheng2022keypoints} the fitted poses are less
accurate than the pre-fit ones ($94.8$ against $90.7$\,mm MPJPE, aligned at the
hip midpoint), and the loss is at the knees and ankles.
Finally, $p{=}0.010$ is uncorrected over the eight strata scored at this
noise level, and the splits of Table~\ref{tab:dyn} were chosen after that
result.

\textbf{Held-out scenes keep the trend and do not confirm the gain over no
pose.} We fixed the stratum, the splits and the tests, and scored the fifteen
models at their final epoch, the only checkpoint kept, on $5{,}648$ validation
scenes that played no part in training, in selecting an epoch or in choosing
the stratum, with $13{,}937$ pedestrians of at least $200$ returns. Own pose is ahead of a stranger's by $2.1\%$ ($p{=}0.12$, CI
$[-5.0,+0.8]$), against $3.3\%$ ($p{=}0.045$) on the original scenes in the
same pass. On the $13{,}350$ with $1$\,s of future, over the quartiles of the
tracker's velocity error, this reads
$0.0$, $-1.4$, $-1.7$ and $-4.9\%$, a trend of $4.9$ points ($p{<}0.001$).
Against no pose the trend is $4.4$ points ($p{=}0.017$), but the gain is not
confirmed: $+0.9\%$ ($p{=}0.67$, CI $[-4.3,+6.0]$), and $-0.1\%$ on the
original scenes at the final epoch, where a stranger's pose costs $3.3\%$
($p{=}0.035$). Such comparisons between architectures are unstable at a fixed
epoch (Sec.~\ref{sec:real}), and the best-epoch rule behind the $2.1\%$ of
Table~\ref{tab:dyn} need not be neutral either: from it the pose arms lose
$3.1$ and $3.3\%$ to the final epoch and the no-pose arm $1.4\%$. On a causal track
own pose is worth more than a stranger's where the tracker is wrong. That it
is worth more than no pose is not established.

\section{Why Pose Does Not Pay on Offboard Tracks}
\label{sec:why}

\subsection{What the oracle ladder measures}
\label{sec:floor}
The content test bounds what pose is worth, and the oracle of
Sec.~\ref{sec:poscontrol} shows the branch can carry information. Neither says
what kind of information this pathway turns into accuracy, so we degrade
the oracle.

Each level of Table~\ref{tab:floor} feeds the branch the same direction-only
oracle, the bearing of the agent's own future displacement at $3$ and $8$\,s,
corrupted by a fixed angular error. Each is labelled by the median
bearing error actually delivered: $0.6745\sigma$ by construction, since the
corruption is a fixed per-agent rotation, and measured to within $0.7^\circ$ of
it on $11{,}328$ scored agents. Displacement magnitude is withheld, so the
channel carries direction alone. The error is drawn once per pedestrian, not per
epoch, as a real estimator's error is a property of the observation.

At $25$k scenes every level converts, and the gain stays large: $-34.50\%$ with an
exact bearing, $-19.77\%$ at $5.0^\circ$, $-15.68\%$ at $10.0^\circ$ and
$-13.57\%$ at $15.1^\circ$, all at $p\le0.0016$ against minimum detectable
effects of $1.4$ to $2.9$\,pp, then $-10.39\%$ at $24.9^\circ$ ($p{=}0.003$, CI
$[-12.9,-7.9]$) and $-6.92\%$ at $44.4^\circ$ ($p{=}0.013$, CI $[-10.3,-3.5]$),
a fifth of the exact gain (Table~\ref{tab:floor}). The decay is not a single
exponential. Two exponentials fit all six levels to $0.3$\,pp, a fast term of
$15.5\%$ with a half-life of $2.1^\circ$ and a slow term of $19.0\%$ with a
half-life of $30^\circ$ ($1.6$ to $3.0^\circ$ and $24$ to $51^\circ$ across the
three seeds). No level lies between $0^\circ$ and $5^\circ$, so the fast
half-life is bounded more than measured: any value from $0.5^\circ$ to
$3^\circ$ fits within $1$\,pp, and $4^\circ$ misses by $1.5$\,pp.

\textbf{The angular axis is not an acceptance threshold.} It is tempting to read
$\theta$ as the accuracy a cue must reach. The ladder refutes that reading
itself. The baseline already receives the labelled box heading, as
a per-timestep sine and cosine pair for every agent (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}), and that
heading estimates the same future displacement bearing at $3.9^\circ$ in
median, better than the $15.1^\circ$ channel at every percentile
($9.3^\circ$ against $25.8^\circ$ at the third quartile, $20.1^\circ$ against
$36.3^\circ$ at the ninetieth). A channel uniformly less accurate than an input
the model already has still buys $-13.6\%$, and the only content it has is the
realised future.

\textbf{The slow term is future-side mode selection.} The two terms behave
differently in ways that identify them.
Per-agent records at each run's own best epoch, paired between arms on
$16{,}486$ agents and averaged over seeds, put the slow term at the end of
the trajectory: the $15.1^\circ$ channel keeps $25\%$ of the exact oracle's error
reduction at $2$\,s, $40\%$ at $8$\,s and $54\%$ at the endpoint. The selection reading predicts more: corrupting the bearing should barely affect which of the
six modes is right. It does not. The gap between the top-ranked mode and the best
of six, which is what the forecaster loses by choosing wrongly, falls from $0.67$\,m to
$0.26$\,m with an exact oracle, and still to $0.29$\,m at $15.1^\circ$: the
corrupted channel closes $92\%$ of the perfect oracle's selection gap while
recovering $39\%$ of its \minADE. A coarse future bearing says which way the
agent went, and only a precise one refines where along that way, which a few
degrees of error destroy.

\minADE takes the best of six modes whatever their rank, so selection reaches it
only through the decoder's scoring step, which keeps six of $64$ intention
queries: a better ranking changes which six survive.
That is also where a pose effect could hide from our headline metric, so we
score the ranking metrics directly on the $25$k arms. They are null as well:
against a stranger's pose, top-$1$ ADE is $-2.07\%$ ($p{=}0.11$, MDE
$3.7$\,pp), best-mode rank $-0.93\%$ ($p{=}0.22$) and endpoint mode spread
$+0.17\%$ ($p{=}0.53$), over the same $16{,}486$ agents.

\subsection{Why body facing does not pay}
\label{sec:belowfloor}
The oracle converts a bearing read off the realised future. Body facing is not
that: it is an observation-time estimate of a quantity the model already
measures. Three properties of it, on the population the ladder scores, say what
is left for it to contribute.

It is a noisier copy of an existing input. The box heading estimates the
future displacement bearing at $3.9^\circ$ ($+3$\,s) and $7.1^\circ$ ($+8$\,s)
in median, while SMPL facing reaches $12.3^\circ$ ($n{=}5{,}824$) and $15.1^\circ$
($n{=}6{,}206$). Box heading is the better estimate at every percentile we
measured, the two differ by $10.0^\circ$ in per-agent median, and both derive
from the same returns. The SMPL forward axis is calibrated against the
box heading before either is measured.

Most of its accuracy is the trajectory restated. $95.5\%$ of the scored
agents move, and for a mover the pipeline snaps root orientation to the direction
of travel whenever the raw estimate deviates by more than $90^\circ$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}). Movers sit at $11.9^\circ$, while the $262$ stationary
agents, the only ones whose facing their velocity does not already imply, sit
at $41.2^\circ$. The pooled median largely measures the snapping.

Where it would be non-redundant, it is worst. On the one case where
velocity carries no directional information (which way a stationary pedestrian
steps off), SMPL facing predicts onset direction at roughly twice chance over
the $1{,}705$ onset events among the $19{,}939$ stationary pedestrians of the whole validation split: median error $44.2^\circ$, $50.6\%$ within
$45^\circ$ (chance $90^\circ$, $25\%$). That is informative, and not an
artifact of our axis fitting: the box heading
estimates the same quantity at $25.2^\circ$, and paired on the same events SMPL
is $7.3^\circ$ worse in median (Wilcoxon $p{=}2.6{\times}10^{-43}$). Nor does
pose take over where perception degrades: over those events the gap
favours the box heading in all ten testable strata ($p<10^{-5}$ each) and
widens with range, to $+32.9^\circ$ at $41$ to $75$\,m, because both derive
from the same returns.

Facing is therefore redundant where it is accurate and inaccurate where it would
be non-redundant, and in neither case is it the selection signal the
oracle supplies. What survives it is what the content test measures directly:
$+0.04\%$ at $25$k scenes, bounded to $\pm1.7\%$.

\textbf{Removing the redundant input does not rescue it.} Redundancy says why
little is left for facing to add, but it is not by itself a mechanism that blocks
conversion. We therefore delete the box-heading channel, retrain all arms and repeat
the content test at $579$ scenes and eight seeds. No benefit appears. The
difference in content effect between the two conditions is $+2.9$\,pp on the
$13{,}006$ moving agents, the wrong sign for a benefit that redundancy
had been masking, and $-0.6$\,pp on the stationary ones ($p{=}0.65$). Deleting the channel
costs the no-pose baseline only $1.5\%$ on movers, because velocity stays in the
input, so there is little room for facing to fill.

\textbf{A frame-rotation control, not a degraded heading.} The heading the
baseline receives is an offboard label too (Sec.~\ref{sec:onboard}), so it may
be better than a deployed tracker's, and facing might pay only where it is
worse. Deleting the channel cannot test that: MTR's agent-centric frame, and
the intention anchors defined in it, stay aligned to the clean heading. We
rotate that frame by a fixed per-agent error $\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$, which
frame, anchors and map inherit, and repeat the content test at $25$k scenes
and three seeds (Table~\ref{tab:onboard}). The no-pose baseline loses $5.8\%$
at $\sigma{=}15^\circ$ and $12.6\%$ at $30^\circ$, and pose recovers none of
it: $-0.14\%$ ($p{=}0.90$) and $-0.11\%$ ($p{=}0.93$) against shuffled. This
does not test a degraded heading. The per-timestep heading channel stays the
clean heading expressed in the rotated frame, so the model can read the added
error, and the pose root stays in world coordinates, so the body cannot state
the correction. Whether facing pays under an onboard-quality heading is
untested here. The clean condition reproduces the main results to within $0.4\%$.

\subsection{A general condition}
Two results locate where pose could have helped. At $2$\,s, \minADE is
$0.097$\,m at $25$k scenes, and a perfect future-direction oracle through the same branch removes
only $1.4$\,cm of it ($-14.7\%$): in absolute terms there is almost nothing for
any cue to win. At long
horizons the residual is dominated by destination and intent, which body
configuration does not disclose.

In general form, and offered as a hypothesis from one modality on one dataset
rather than a law: \emph{an auxiliary modality improves a forecast only where the
baseline's own residual leaves room in absolute terms, where that residual is not
dominated by intent the modality cannot observe, and where the modality carries
information about the cue that the model's existing inputs do not already carry.}
The third clause is not a comparison of marginal accuracies:
Sec.~\ref{sec:floor} converts a channel less accurate at every percentile than an
input the model already has. What disqualifies facing is that its error is
shared with the input it would supplement
(Sec.~\ref{sec:belowfloor}). Sec.~\ref{sec:onboard} tests the clause from the
other side, and shows it has a trap. A cue whose pipeline was fitted to the input
it supplements carries that input, and looks informative exactly when the input
is degraded. Redundancy is a property of a modality together with the inputs
beside it, and an offboard-labelled benchmark sets those inputs at offboard
quality.

\section{Practice and Limitations}
For an AV stack, three consequences follow. First, before integrating an
auxiliary modality, measure how much of its cue it explains beyond the
inputs the model already receives, take those inputs at the quality a vehicle
will have, and ask what the cue's own pipeline was fitted to. None of this costs
training: our fitted poses predict the labelled speed with $R^2{=}0.94$, which
flags the leak before any model is trained. Second, an oracle ladder is worth its few
runs as a positive control and an upper bound on the pathway, if its MDE is
below the effect one would act on (Sec.~\ref{sec:floor}). Third, the cost of an extra
branch (${\sim}1.6\%$ here, at the best-epoch operating point) is easily
mistaken for a modality effect in either direction. A shuffled-pose control
separates the two, and early fusion avoids the cost while still reading the
channel.

\textbf{Limitations.} We test one forecaster, one dataset and one pose
estimator, the model sees pedestrians and map only, so pose cannot act through
interaction with vehicles and the baseline is not comparable with published
WOMD results, and we do not model pose--pose interaction. The largest scale we train is $25$k of the $299{,}220$
exported scenes, a compute limit, and the ladders run at $n{=}3$ per level, five
for track noise. We did not test a degraded heading: the frame-rotation control
leaves the clean heading readable, and the default pose arm carries its root in
world coordinates (Sec.~\ref{sec:belowfloor}). The onboard track is simulated
(Sec.~\ref{sec:onboard}). The pre-fit poses are still estimated from points cropped by
offboard boxes, the pre-fit control covers the $30$\,cm level only, and we have
not run either through early fusion. The null on offboard tracks is measured
with fitted poses. Pre-fit poses were not trained on clean tracks, and the
nearest evidence is the quartile of Table~\ref{tab:dyn} in which the tracker is
nearly right, where they gain nothing over no pose. WOMD ships camera data only as quantised tokens, so a
camera-based pose or gaze estimate cannot be tested at this scale.

\section{Conclusion}
Body pose, recovered from LiDAR, does not improve pedestrian trajectory
forecasting on WOMD's offboard tracks. The null is controlled against a
shuffled-pose baseline and holds across scale, representation and integration,
and an oracle through the same branch rules out a dead pathway. Under a
simulated onboard tracker own pose beats a stranger's by $5.3\%$, and three
quarters of that is the offboard label, carried in by a pose pipeline that fits
the gait to the track. Poses that never saw the track gain a non-significant
$1.3\%$ overall and $3.5\%$ on the best-resolved bodies. On held-out scenes
that is $2.1\%$ ($p{=}0.12$), it grows with the tracker's error, and a gain
over no pose is not confirmed. The results are one mechanism: a cue pays only for what it carries
beyond the model's existing inputs, an offboard track leaves the body nothing to
add, and a cue fitted to a label carries the label.
